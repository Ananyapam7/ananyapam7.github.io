**Overview.** This post studies exact sampling and reparameterization for variational distributions constructed from normalized B-spline mixtures. The main observation is that the mixture CDF is a degree-raised B-spline whose coefficients are cumulative sums of the mixture weights. This gives an exact inverse-CDF sampler and implicit pathwise gradients without a [Concrete relaxation](#ref-maddison-2017) or an inner Markov chain. In numerical experiments, randomized stratification reduced gradient variance by approximately 84 times compared with independent sampling at equal inverse-CDF cost. I also examine why the ELBO Hessian is not generally a measure of estimator precision, and how penalty scaling distinguishes variance-based smoothing from population model correction.

One of the recurring difficulties in variational inference is that making an approximate posterior more expressive usually makes it harder to sample from and differentiate through. Gaussian variational families are convenient largely because they admit a simple reparameterization: we sample a standard normal variable and transform it using parameters supplied by the encoder. Once we move beyond Gaussians, finding an equally convenient representation becomes more difficult.

I was looking at a variational family constructed from mixtures of normalized B-spline densities, introduced in the spline-based variational inference work of [Shao, Yu and Feng (2024)](#ref-shao-2024). The appeal of this family is fairly clear. B-splines are locally supported, their shape can be controlled through mixture weights, and evaluating the resulting density is inexpensive. The less convenient part is sampling. The original approach uses Metropolis–Hastings together with a [Concrete relaxation](#ref-maddison-2017), which makes differentiation possible but also introduces additional computation and a discrepancy between the distribution being optimized and the distribution actually sampled.

It turns out that this difficulty can be avoided without changing the variational family. The main observation is that the CDF of a normalized B-spline mixture has an especially simple representation: it is another B-spline, one degree higher, whose coefficients are cumulative sums of the original mixture weights. This allows us to sample by CDF inversion and obtain pathwise derivatives through implicit differentiation. The resulting sampler also makes randomized stratification particularly effective; in the numerical experiment considered here, using 32 stratified samples reduced gradient variance by a factor of about 84 compared with independent sampling at the same inverse-CDF cost.

While studying this construction, I also looked at the question of automatically selecting a roughness penalty for the spline weights. That led to a somewhat different issue. Although the ELBO is concave in the mixture weights, its Hessian is entirely determined by the entropy of the variational distribution. The target distribution contributes no curvature. As a consequence, directly using this Hessian in a smoothing-parameter update can lead to pathological behaviour, and more generally, it is important to distinguish regularization that reduces estimation variance from regularization that changes the population optimum.

I’ll develop these two parts in turn, beginning with the sampling problem.

## Key mathematical results

The analysis establishes several useful properties of spline variational families.

**Exact inverse-CDF sampling.** For a normalized B-spline mixture <span markdown="0">\(q_\gamma(e)=\sum_k\gamma_k b_k(e)\)</span>, the CDF is a degree-raised B-spline with cumulative-sum coefficients:



<div markdown="0">
\[F_\gamma(e)=\sum_{m=0}^K\Gamma_m\bar B_{m,\rho+1}(e), \qquad \Gamma_m=\sum_{k=1}^m\gamma_k.\]
</div>



This provides exact sampling through <span markdown="0">\(e=F_\gamma^{-1}(u)\)</span>, where <span markdown="0">\(u\sim U(0,1)\)</span>.

**Implicit reparameterization gradients.** Differentiating the inverse-CDF equation gives



<div markdown="0">
\[\frac{\partial e}{\partial\gamma_k}=-\frac{\int_0^e b_k(s)\,ds}{q_\gamma(e)}.\]
</div>



This avoids differentiating through the numerical root solver and provides pathwise gradients without a [Concrete relaxation](#ref-maddison-2017).

**The geometry of the ELBO.** For fixed support and basis functions, the ELBO Hessian in mixture-weight coordinates satisfies



<div markdown="0">
\[\nabla_\gamma^2\mathcal L_{\mathrm{ELBO}}=-\mathcal I(\gamma).\]
</div>



The target-density contribution is linear in the weights, so this curvature comes entirely from entropy. It should not automatically be interpreted as estimator precision.

**Variance smoothing versus population correction.** In the exact <span markdown="0">\(T=2\)</span> [IWAE](#ref-burda-2016) experiment, the RISE-optimal penalty grows approximately as <span markdown="0">\(\lambda_n^*\approx 0.17n\)</span>. Its effect therefore persists on the population-objective scale, rather than vanishing with estimation uncertainty.

## The spline mixture

Consider a collection of B-spline basis functions <span markdown="0">\(B_{k,\rho}\)</span> of degree <span markdown="0">\(\rho\)</span>, defined on a clamped knot sequence over <span markdown="0">\([0,1]\)</span>. A standard B-spline basis function is nonnegative but does not generally integrate to one. We can turn each basis function into a probability density by normalizing it:



<div markdown="0">
\[a_k=\int_0^1 B_{k,\rho}(e)\,de =\frac{t_{k+\rho+1}-t_k}{\rho+1}, \qquad b_k(e)=\frac{B_{k,\rho}(e)}{a_k}.\]
</div>



Given weights <span markdown="0">\(\gamma=(\gamma_1,\ldots,\gamma_K)\)</span> on the probability simplex, define



<div markdown="0">
\[q_\gamma(e)=\sum_{k=1}^K\gamma_k b_k(e), \qquad e\in[0,1].\]
</div>



This is a density because each <span markdown="0">\(b_k\)</span> integrates to one and the mixture weights sum to one. Increasing a particular weight puts more mass in the region supported by its corresponding basis function, while the overlap between neighbouring B-splines allows the overall density to vary smoothly.

{% include spline_figure.html id="mixture" path="assets/img/spline-vi/mixture.png" alt="Normalized B-spline components and three mixtures of those components." caption="Normalized cubic B-spline components, and three mixtures of those components. Moving weight to the left or the right shifts the density without leaving the spline family." %}

In variational inference we can use a neural network to predict these weights, together with location and scale parameters. For a latent variable <span markdown="0">\(z\)</span>, the construction takes the form



<div markdown="0">
\[z=\mu(x)+\sigma(x)e, \qquad e\sim q_{\gamma(x)}.\]
</div>



For several latent coordinates, we can use a separate spline mixture for each coordinate, giving a mean-field family. The mixture weights are conveniently parameterized through logits, <span markdown="0">\(\gamma=\operatorname{softmax}(\eta)\)</span>, so positivity and normalization are automatic.

Sampling from such a mixture is easy if differentiability is not a concern. We first draw an index <span markdown="0">\(C\)</span> from a categorical distribution with probabilities <span markdown="0">\(\gamma\)</span>, then sample from <span markdown="0">\(b_C\)</span>. This produces exactly the required distribution, but the discrete component choice is not directly differentiable with respect to the weights.

The [Concrete relaxation](#ref-maddison-2017) provides a way around this by replacing the categorical selection with a differentiable vector of approximately one-hot weights. If <span markdown="0">\(w_k\sim b_k\)</span> independently, the relaxed sample is



<div markdown="0">
\[e_\Lambda=\sum_{k=1}^K u_k(\Lambda)w_k,\]
</div>



where <span markdown="0">\(u(\Lambda)\)</span> is a [Concrete](#ref-maddison-2017) random vector and <span markdown="0">\(\Lambda\)</span> is its temperature. As the temperature approaches zero, the relaxation approaches a categorical choice. At any positive temperature, however, the sample is a convex combination of draws from several components rather than a draw from one selected component.

That changes the distribution in a systematic way. For example, if <span markdown="0">\(m_k=\mathbb E[w_k]\)</span>, an expansion around zero temperature gives



<div markdown="0">
\[\operatorname{Var}(e_\Lambda) =\operatorname{Var}(e_0)-\Lambda\Bigg[ \sum_k\gamma_k(1-\gamma_k)\operatorname{Var}(w_k) +\frac12\sum_{k\ne j} \gamma_k\gamma_j(m_k-m_j)^2 \Bigg]+O(\Lambda^2).\]
</div>



The first-order change in the mean is zero, but the first-order change in variance is nonpositive. In a numerical check using one million draws with common random numbers, the observed slope of the second moment was <span markdown="0">\(-0.10672\)</span>, compared with the theoretical value <span markdown="0">\(-0.10775\)</span>. The relative difference was approximately <span markdown="0">\(0.96\%\)</span>.

{% include spline_figure.html id="relaxation" path="assets/img/spline-vi/relaxation.png" alt="Exact mixture density compared with a Concrete relaxation, and variance against temperature." caption="[Concrete relaxation](#ref-maddison-2017) of the example mixture. Left: the exact density against relaxed samples at two temperatures. Right: the variance falls as the temperature rises, and the dashed line is the first-order expansion." %}

This does not mean that continuous relaxations are inherently unsuitable. They can be quite useful when an exact reparameterization is unavailable. But here the underlying distribution has enough structure that the relaxation turns out to be unnecessary.

## The CDF of a Normalized B-Spline Mixture

The key fact comes from the antiderivative formula for B-splines.

If we integrate a degree-<span markdown="0">\(\rho\)</span> B-spline expansion, the result can be represented as a degree-<span markdown="0">\((\rho+1)\)</span> spline on an appropriately extended knot sequence. Its new coefficients are cumulative sums of the original coefficients multiplied by their corresponding basis integrals.

For the normalized mixture, those integrals cancel with the normalizing constants. If we write



<div markdown="0">
\[F_\gamma(e)=\int_0^e q_\gamma(s)\,ds,\]
</div>



and define



<div markdown="0">
\[\Gamma_0=0, \qquad \Gamma_m=\sum_{k=1}^m\gamma_k,\]
</div>



then



<div markdown="0">
\[\boxed{ F_\gamma(e)= \sum_{m=0}^K \Gamma_m\bar B_{m,\rho+1}(e). }\]
</div>



Here <span markdown="0">\(\bar B_{m,\rho+1}\)</span> denotes the degree-raised B-spline basis with one additional repeated boundary knot at each end.

The point is that we do not need to integrate the basis numerically. We only need to take the cumulative sum of the mixture weights and evaluate a spline of degree one higher. For example, if the mixture weights are



<div markdown="0">
\[\gamma=(0.1,0.2,0.4,0.2,0.1),\]
</div>



then the CDF coefficients are simply



<div markdown="0">
\[\Gamma=(0,0.1,0.3,0.7,0.9,1).\]
</div>



The final coefficient is one because the mixture weights sum to one. If all weights are positive, the density is positive throughout the interior of its support, and the CDF is strictly increasing there.

{% include spline_figure.html id="cdf" path="assets/img/spline-vi/cdf.png" alt="Example spline mixture density and its degree-raised CDF." caption="The example mixture and its CDF. The degree-raised spline matches the integral of the plotted density." %}

This gives an immediate sampling procedure. Draw <span markdown="0">\(u\sim U(0,1)\)</span> and solve



<div markdown="0">
\[F_\gamma(e)=u.\]
</div>



The solution <span markdown="0">\(e=F_\gamma^{-1}(u)\)</span> has exactly the desired mixture distribution, apart from the numerical tolerance used to solve the equation. In particular, we no longer need to draw a categorical component or introduce any relaxation.

The inverse can be computed efficiently because the CDF is polynomial within each knot span. We first locate the relevant span using the CDF values at the knots, initialize the root using linear interpolation, and apply Newton's method with a bisection safeguard. Only <span markdown="0">\(\rho+1\)</span> basis functions are nonzero locally, so evaluating the polynomial at each iteration does not require traversing the full spline basis.

There is still the question of differentiation. We could, in principle, backpropagate through every iteration of the root solver, but doing so is unnecessary.

## Implicit Reparameterization Gradients for B-Spline Mixtures

The inverse sample is defined implicitly by



<div markdown="0">
\[F_\gamma(e)=u.\]
</div>



Keeping <span markdown="0">\(u\)</span> fixed and differentiating with respect to the mixture weight <span markdown="0">\(\gamma_k\)</span> gives



<div markdown="0">
\[\frac{\partial F_\gamma(e)}{\partial\gamma_k} + \frac{\partial F_\gamma(e)}{\partial e} \frac{\partial e}{\partial\gamma_k} =0.\]
</div>



Since



<div markdown="0">
\[\frac{\partial F_\gamma(e)}{\partial e}=q_\gamma(e)\]
</div>



and



<div markdown="0">
\[\frac{\partial F_\gamma(e)}{\partial\gamma_k} = \int_0^e b_k(s)\,ds,\]
</div>



we obtain



<div markdown="0">
\[\boxed{ \frac{\partial e}{\partial\gamma_k} = -\frac{\bar{\mathcal B}_k(e)}{q_\gamma(e)}, \qquad \bar{\mathcal B}_k(e)=\int_0^e b_k(s)\,ds. }\]
</div>



The derivatives with respect to the unconstrained logits then follow by applying the softmax Jacobian. This is an instance of implicit reparameterization, as developed by [Figurnov, Mohamed and Mnih (2018)](#ref-figurnov-2018), but the spline representation means that both the CDF and its parameter derivative can be evaluated without numerical integration.

In the implementation, the root search is performed without tracking its iterations through automatic differentiation. Once a sufficiently accurate root <span markdown="0">\(e_0\)</span> has been obtained, we make one differentiable Newton correction,



<div markdown="0">
\[e=e_0- \frac{F_\gamma(e_0)-u} {q_\gamma(e_0)_{\mathrm{detach}}},\]
</div>



where the denominator is detached from the computational graph. The numerical value changes only by the remaining root error, while the backward pass uses the required implicit derivative.

Under standard differentiability and domination assumptions, expectations can be written as



<div markdown="0">
\[\mathbb E_{e\sim q_\gamma}[h(e,\gamma)] = \mathbb E_{u\sim U(0,1)} \left[h(F_\gamma^{-1}(u),\gamma)\right],\]
</div>



and differentiation can be moved inside the expectation. The resulting pathwise gradient is therefore unbiased.

In the numerical checks, the maximum CDF round-trip error in double precision was <span markdown="0">\(7.8\times10^{-16}\)</span>, and the implicit gradient agreed with finite differences to <span markdown="0">\(7.3\times10^{-11}\)</span>. A probability-integral-transform check with 200,000 samples gave a Kolmogorov–Smirnov <span markdown="0">\(p\)</span>-value of <span markdown="0">\(0.985\)</span>. Across 10,000 independent gradient experiments, no component rejected zero bias after Bonferroni correction.

{% include spline_figure.html id="sampler" path="assets/img/spline-vi/sampler.png" alt="Inverse-CDF samples beside the implicit derivatives with respect to the mixture weights." caption="Inverse-CDF samples from the example mixture, and the implicit derivatives with respect to each mixture weight where the density is not small." %}

The theoretical result is exact for the stated mixture family; the numerical tests establish that the implemented inversion and differentiation are accurate to the reported tolerances.

## Variance Reduction with Randomized Stratification

Having a differentiable inverse-CDF sampler is useful on its own, but it also changes how we can approach Monte Carlo variance reduction. Instead of working with a discrete mixture indicator and separate component draws, the entire randomness of a one-dimensional sample is represented by a single uniform variable.

Suppose we want to estimate a gradient using <span markdown="0">\(S\)</span> samples. Ordinary Monte Carlo draws <span markdown="0">\(u_1,\ldots,u_S\)</span> independently from <span markdown="0">\(U(0,1)\)</span>. A perfectly valid outcome is that several samples fall into one narrow region of the interval while another region receives none. The estimator is still unbiased, but this uneven coverage can contribute substantially to its variance.

Randomized stratification avoids that particular source of randomness. Divide <span markdown="0">\([0,1]\)</span> into <span markdown="0">\(S\)</span> equal intervals and draw one uniform point from each:



<div markdown="0">
\[u_s=\frac{s-1+v_s}{S}, \qquad v_s\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}U(0,1).\]
</div>



Each point is random within its assigned interval. Averaging the transformed gradient evaluations remains unbiased, since the strata partition the integration domain with equal weights.

The reason this works well with inverse-CDF sampling is that the transformation is monotone. Covering the uniform interval evenly means that we also cover the quantile range of the spline distribution evenly. For sufficiently regular gradient integrands, this can remove a large amount of sampling variability.

An interesting detail is that the corresponding estimator-precision improvements cannot be inferred simply by taking reciprocals of these scalar variance ratios. Gradient covariance is a matrix, and different sampling schemes can reduce variability by different amounts in different directions. This becomes relevant when we consider smoothing.

## The ELBO Hessian and Fisher Information

Suppose we also want to control how irregular the spline density can become. For cubic splines, a natural roughness penalty is based on integrated squared second derivatives:



<div markdown="0">
\[R(\gamma)=\gamma^\top P\gamma, \qquad P_{k\ell} = \int_0^1 b_k''(e)b_\ell''(e)\,de.\]
</div>



Choosing <span markdown="0">\(\lambda\)</span>, the coefficient multiplying this penalty, is the usual smoothing-parameter problem. A natural first thought is to borrow ideas from penalized likelihood estimation, where Hessian or Fisher information matrices play a central role in measuring how precisely model parameters are identified.

To see whether that approach works here, consider the ELBO for a fixed spline support and basis:



<div markdown="0">
\[\mathcal L(\gamma) = \int q_\gamma(e)\log\widetilde p(e)\,de - \int q_\gamma(e)\log q_\gamma(e)\,de.\]
</div>



The first term depends on the target density, while the second is the entropy of the variational distribution. Because <span markdown="0">\(q_\gamma\)</span> is linear in <span markdown="0">\(\gamma\)</span>, the first term is also linear in <span markdown="0">\(\gamma\)</span>. Its second derivative with respect to the mixture weights vanishes.

Differentiating the entropy term twice gives



<div markdown="0">
\[\nabla_\gamma^2\mathcal L(\gamma) = -\int_0^1 \frac{b(e)b(e)^\top}{q_\gamma(e)}\,de.\]
</div>



Writing



<div markdown="0">
\[\mathcal I(\gamma) = \int_0^1 \frac{b(e)b(e)^\top}{q_\gamma(e)}\,de,\]
</div>



we have



<div markdown="0">
\[\boxed{ \nabla_\gamma^2\mathcal L(\gamma) = -\mathcal I(\gamma). }\]
</div>



This is the Fisher information matrix of the mixture in weight coordinates, restricted as necessary to the simplex tangent space. The identity shows that the ELBO is concave in the weights and that the corresponding KL divergence is convex.

What is less obvious is what this identity does *not* tell us. The matrix <span markdown="0">\(\mathcal I(\gamma)\)</span> depends on the fitted mixture, but it has no direct contribution from the target density or the observation model. The target affects the location of the optimum, not the Hessian formula itself.

Consequently, the curvature of the ELBO cannot by itself tell us how much information the observations contain about fine-scale features of the fitted density. It describes the local geometry of the variational family, which is not necessarily the same as the sampling precision of the parameter estimator.

The distinction is easy to overlook because, in a correctly specified likelihood model, expected curvature and score covariance coincide under the [Fisher information identity](#ref-white-1982). That equality is not available automatically for a variational objective.

## Why Fisher-Based [Fellner–Schall](#ref-wood-2017) Smoothing Can Diverge

Consider a [Fellner–Schall](#ref-wood-2017)-type update of the form



<div markdown="0">
\[\lambda_{\mathrm{new}} = \frac{\operatorname{edf}(\lambda)-\nu} {R(\gamma_\lambda)},\]
</div>



where <span markdown="0">\(\operatorname{edf}\)</span> denotes effective degrees of freedom and <span markdown="0">\(\nu\)</span> is the number of unpenalized directions. The idea is to balance the model's remaining flexibility against the roughness of the fitted solution.

If we insert the ELBO Fisher information directly into this construction, the update can behave badly even when the underlying numerical calculations are stable.

The mechanism becomes clear in a quadratic approximation. Whiten the local curvature and diagonalize the penalty, with positive generalized eigenvalues <span markdown="0">\(d_k\)</span>. If <span markdown="0">\(a_k\)</span> are the corresponding coordinates of the unpenalized solution, the penalized coefficients are



<div markdown="0">
\[\beta_k(\lambda)=\frac{a_k}{1+\lambda d_k}.\]
</div>



For the direct Fisher construction, the identity



<div markdown="0">
\[\gamma^\top\mathcal I(\gamma)\gamma=1\]
</div>



implies that the whitened coordinates satisfy <span markdown="0">\(\|a\|^2=1\)</span>. The difference between the effective-degrees-of-freedom term and the scaled roughness is then



<div markdown="0">
\[\{\operatorname{edf}(\lambda)-\nu\} -\lambda R(\lambda) = \sum_{d_k>0} \frac{1+\lambda d_k(1-a_k^2)} {(1+\lambda d_k)^2}.\]
</div>



Since <span markdown="0">\(|a_k|\leq1\)</span>, every term in this sum is positive. Thus, within this quadratic construction, the update increases <span markdown="0">\(\lambda\)</span> at every finite value.

{% include spline_figure.html id="runaway" path="assets/img/spline-vi/runaway.png" alt="Whitened coefficients shrinking with the penalty, and a Fellner–Schall residual that stays positive." caption="Quadratic Fisher construction. The whitened coefficients shrink as the penalty grows, and the residual that drives the smoothing update stays positive." %}

The divergence is a consequence of the mixture geometry, not evidence that the data contain no non-affine structure. Rescaling the roughness matrix changes the numerical scale of <span markdown="0">\(\lambda\)</span>, but does not resolve the underlying problem.

For variance-based smoothing, what we actually need is the uncertainty of the fitted estimator. That requires accounting for the variability of the stochastic gradients.

## From curvature to estimator precision

Consider a sample-average objective



<div markdown="0">
\[\widehat{\mathcal L}_S(\eta) = \frac1S\sum_{s=1}^S\ell(\eta;u_s),\]
</div>



with population maximizer <span markdown="0">\(\eta_0\)</span>. Define



<div markdown="0">
\[A=-\mathbb E[\nabla_\eta^2\ell(\eta_0;u)]\]
</div>



and



<div markdown="0">
\[B=\operatorname{Cov} \left[\nabla_\eta\ell(\eta_0;u)\right].\]
</div>



Here <span markdown="0">\(A\)</span> describes curvature, while <span markdown="0">\(B\)</span> measures gradient variability. Under the usual conditions for [M-estimation](#ref-white-1982),



<div markdown="0">
\[\sqrt S(\widehat\eta_S-\eta_0) \Rightarrow N(0,A^{-1}BA^{-1}).\]
</div>



The precision corresponding to this asymptotic covariance is therefore



<div markdown="0">
\[M_\eta=SAB^+A\]
</div>



on the identifiable parameter space. The pseudoinverse is relevant because the softmax logits have a common-shift direction that does not change the mixture weights.

For a shared encoder fitted to <span markdown="0">\(n\)</span> independent observations, there are two sources of stochastic variation: differences between observations and Monte Carlo noise conditional on an observation. If <span markdown="0">\(g(x,u)\)</span> is the stochastic score and <span markdown="0">\(S\)</span> independent Monte Carlo draws are used per observation, the relevant covariance is



<div markdown="0">
\[B_S= \operatorname{Var}_x\{\mathbb E[g(x,u)\mid x]\} + \frac1S\mathbb E_x \{\operatorname{Var}[g(x,u)\mid x]\}.\]
</div>



The corresponding precision is



<div markdown="0">
\[M_w=nAB_S^+A.\]
</div>



This is the matrix that should enter a smoothing rule intended to control estimator variance, subject to the appropriate regularity and identifiability conditions.

There is also a connection back to sampling. A sampling design that reduces <span markdown="0">\(B_S\)</span> improves the precision of the estimated parameters, potentially changing the appropriate amount of smoothing. The variance reductions from stratification are therefore relevant beyond the immediate problem of obtaining less noisy gradients.

However, even an accurately estimated sandwich precision cannot answer every regularization question. It can help determine how much smoothing is warranted by estimation uncertainty, but it cannot decide whether we should deliberately change the population optimum to improve some other measure of approximation quality.

That distinction turned out to be important in the numerical experiments.

## Distinguishing Variance Smoothing from Population Model Correction

Write the penalized estimator on the average-objective scale as



<div markdown="0">
\[\widehat w_{n,\lambda} = \arg\max_w \left\{ \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\ell_i(w) - \frac{\lambda}{2n}R(w) \right\}.\]
</div>



The quantity controlling the contribution of the penalty relative to the empirical objective is <span markdown="0">\(\lambda/n\)</span>.

Suppose first that <span markdown="0">\(\lambda_n=O(1)\)</span> as the sample size grows. Then <span markdown="0">\(\lambda_n/n\to0\)</span>, so the penalty disappears from the limiting population objective. This is the usual behaviour of a regularization term whose role is to stabilize a finite-sample estimate.

Now suppose instead that



<div markdown="0">
\[\frac{\lambda_n}{n}\to c>0.\]
</div>



Under appropriate convergence assumptions, the limiting estimator maximizes



<div markdown="0">
\[\mathbb E[\ell_i(w)]-\frac c2R(w).\]
</div>



The roughness penalty remains present even with arbitrarily large samples. In other words, the regularization is not merely controlling finite-sample uncertainty. It is defining a different population optimization problem.

This suggests a simple way of diagnosing what a risk-optimal penalty is doing: examine how it scales with the sample size. If the oracle penalty grows proportionally to <span markdown="0">\(n\)</span>, the evidence points toward a persistent population-level effect rather than regularization that disappears as estimation uncertainty vanishes.

A regular [Fellner–Schall](#ref-wood-2017) fixed point behaves differently. If the fitted roughness converges to a positive constant and the effective degrees of freedom converge, the fixed-point relation



<div markdown="0">
\[\lambda_nR(\widehat w_n) = \operatorname{edf}_n(\lambda_n)-\nu\]
</div>



implies that <span markdown="0">\(\lambda_n=O(1)\)</span>. Such a selector cannot asymptotically track an oracle satisfying <span markdown="0">\(\lambda_n^*/n\to c>0\)</span>.

This does not make either penalty intrinsically wrong. It means they are optimizing different aspects of the problem.

## A small exact [IWAE](#ref-burda-2016) experiment

To investigate this distinction without introducing Monte Carlo noise into the fitted objective, I used a <span markdown="0">\(T=2\)</span> [importance-weighted variational objective](#ref-burda-2016) evaluated by deterministic quadrature. The variational model had five spline mixture components and a shared affine encoder. Fitting, penalty selection, and evaluation used independent samples, and five seeds were run at each of <span markdown="0">\(n=100,200,400\)</span>.

The external criterion was root integrated squared error,



<div markdown="0">
\[\operatorname{RISE}(q,p) = \left[ \mathbb E_x\int \{q(z\mid x)-p(z\mid x)\}^2\,dz \right]^{1/2}.\]
</div>



This is deliberately different from the [IWAE](#ref-burda-2016) objective. The question was not which penalty maximizes the variational bound, but which penalty gives the smallest density error.

The oracle was selected over 40 logarithmically spaced penalties between 1 and 100. The selected values were interior to the grid, and the five-seed medians were as follows.

This is the signature of a penalty that continues to modify the population objective. The [Fellner–Schall](#ref-wood-2017) penalty grows much more slowly, so the ratio of its penalty to the RISE-optimal penalty decreases as <span markdown="0">\(n\)</span> increases.

There are plausible reasons for the external risk to prefer a permanently penalized solution. In a restricted variational family, the population optimum of a <span markdown="0">\(T=2\)</span> [IWAE](#ref-burda-2016) objective need not be the <span markdown="0">\(L^2\)</span> projection of the true posterior. The [importance-weighted objective](#ref-cremer-2017) has an interpretation involving an augmented or implicit variational distribution, rather than simply reverse KL in the original proposal family.

The affine encoder also introduces its own restriction: even if the spline mixture is flexible for a fixed observation, the shared map <span markdown="0">\(x\mapsto\gamma(x)\)</span> cannot represent every possible pointwise-optimal mixture. A roughness penalty could compensate for either source of misspecification by moving the fitted model toward a density with lower integrated squared error.

The experiment does not distinguish between these two mechanisms, and the sample-size range is limited. What it does show is that the difference between the two selectors is not appropriately described as the variance-based selector failing to recover the risk oracle. The oracle is choosing a different population fit.

As a further check, selecting the penalty using a held-out version of the unpenalized variational objective produced much smaller penalties, between <span markdown="0">\(10^{-6}\)</span> and <span markdown="0">\(0.3\)</span> in the corresponding sweep. That is consistent with the fact that a persistent penalty cannot improve the original population objective over its own unpenalized optimum. It may still improve an external metric such as RISE.

## A useful bound for encoder approximation error

There is another observation about the spline mixture that is worth recording because it affects how approximation error scales with the number of basis functions.

Let



<div markdown="0">
\[\gamma^*=\operatorname{softmax}(\eta^*), \qquad \widehat\gamma=\operatorname{softmax}(\eta^*+\delta).\]
</div>



We want to understand how an error <span markdown="0">\(\delta\)</span> in the encoder logits affects the resulting mixture density.

Both <span markdown="0">\(q_{\widehat\gamma}\)</span> and <span markdown="0">\(q_{\gamma^*}\)</span> are obtained by marginalizing a joint distribution over the component index and the sample, using the same conditional component densities <span markdown="0">\(b_k\)</span>. The data-processing inequality therefore gives



<div markdown="0">
\[\operatorname{KL} (q_{\widehat\gamma}\|q_{\gamma^*}) \leq \operatorname{KL}(\widehat\gamma\|\gamma^*).\]
</div>



The discrete KL between softmax vectors is a Bregman divergence of the log-sum-exp function. Bounding its curvature gives



<div markdown="0">
\[\operatorname{KL} (q_{\widehat\gamma}\|q_{\gamma^*}) \leq \frac18\operatorname{osc}(\delta)^2 \leq \frac12\|\delta\|_\infty^2,\]
</div>



where



<div markdown="0">
\[\operatorname{osc}(\delta) = \max_k\delta_k-\min_k\delta_k.\]
</div>



The useful feature is that the bound contains no explicit factor involving <span markdown="0">\(K\)</span>, the number of spline components.

If approximation error is measured as an absolute error in every mixture weight, dimension-dependent factors can appear because the individual weights typically shrink as the number of bases increases. Measuring error in logits avoids that issue for this part of the approximation argument. Under the relevant approximation and truncation assumptions, the amortization term can therefore be expressed in terms of squared logit error without an additional factor from the number of basis functions.

This does not remove every form of complexity dependence, and it does not imply unlimited smoothness adaptation. In particular, a penalty based on second derivatives is naturally limited to smoothness adaptation up to order two.

The root solver was numerically well behaved in double precision. For the Fisher information calculations, Gauss–Legendre quadrature with 12 nodes per knot span gave relative errors below <span markdown="0">\(2.1\times10^{-14}\)</span> in the tested configurations.

There is a related numerical issue in smoothing calculations. Effective degrees of freedom can be evaluated through



<div markdown="0">
\[\operatorname{edf}(\lambda) = \sum_k\frac{1}{1+\lambda d_k},\]
</div>



where <span markdown="0">\(d_k\)</span> are generalized eigenvalues of the penalty relative to the information matrix. This representation was stable and monotone even at very large penalty values, whereas direct matrix solves could suffer from cancellation.

As a check on the restricted-likelihood calculations, a Gaussian penalized-spline regression produced a smoothing parameter of <span markdown="0">\(0.30652445\)</span>, compared with <span markdown="0">\(0.30652447\)</span> from `mgcv` 1.9-4. That agreement validates the numerical calculation in the regular Gaussian setting; it does not establish that the same information identity holds for a variational objective.

## What this means

The main practical result is that spline mixtures are easier to work with than their sampling formulation initially suggests. Their CDF has a simple representation in terms of cumulative mixture weights, which gives an exact reparameterization without introducing a discrete relaxation or running an inner Markov chain. Combined with implicit differentiation, this provides pathwise gradients for the original variational family rather than for an approximation to it. The numerical experiments also show that the resulting one-dimensional transport is particularly well suited to randomized stratification, with substantially lower gradient variance at the same sampling cost.

There are still computational and modeling trade-offs. Inverse-CDF sampling is more expensive than Gaussian reparameterization, and the experiments here are limited to reference problems rather than large-scale generative models. Moreover, the construction does not change the expressiveness of the underlying variational family: the mean-field assumption and compact support remain. What it does establish is that the difficulties associated with sampling from this family need not be addressed by changing the distribution itself.

The analysis of smoothing parameters raises a somewhat different issue. Although the ELBO Hessian has an exact Fisher-information representation, this curvature comes entirely from the entropy term and cannot, by itself, quantify the sampling uncertainty of the fitted parameters. That uncertainty depends on the covariance of the estimating gradients, which is why the sandwich precision is the relevant object for variance-based smoothing. Even with the correct precision matrix, however, there is an important distinction between reducing estimation variance and deliberately changing the population fit. The exact [IWAE](#ref-burda-2016) experiment illustrates this: the RISE-optimal penalty grows approximately as <span markdown="0">\(\lambda_n^*\approx 0.17n\)</span>, so its effect does not disappear as the sample size increases. It is correcting a population-level mismatch rather than simply stabilizing a noisy estimate.

What I find interesting is that both results come from examining assumptions that are easy to take for granted. The use of a [Concrete relaxation](#ref-maddison-2017) implicitly accepts that changing the sampling distribution is necessary for differentiation, while directly applying a [Fisher-based smoothing rule](#ref-wood-2017) assumes that objective curvature represents estimator precision. Neither assumption holds in the form needed here. In the first case, exploiting the algebraic structure of the spline family removes the approximation altogether. In the second, examining the source of the curvature reveals why a seemingly reasonable automatic smoothing procedure can fail, and why even a statistically appropriate variance-based selector need not agree with an oracle defined by a different loss.

The broader point is not that approximations or regularization should be avoided, but that their effects need to be separated. Sampling error, estimation uncertainty, and population objective mismatch are different problems, even when they ultimately appear through the same quantity, such as the quality of a fitted posterior. For spline variational families, exact transport makes the sampling problem considerably cleaner. It also gives us a better starting point for studying the remaining questions about how accurately the variational parameters can be estimated and what kind of regularization actually improves the approximation we care about.
