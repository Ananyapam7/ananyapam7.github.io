Independent factors are convenient, but cannot express shared variation between unknowns. Giving those factors a common random input can create dependence while preserving simple conditional distributions.

Hierarchical variational models place a mixing distribution over the parameters of simple conditional variational factors. Integrating out that auxiliary variable creates dependence in \(q_\phi\), while leaving the generative model unchanged. Because the resulting marginal variational density may be intractable, an auxiliary inference distribution supplies a usable lower bound. The added hierarchy is in the approximation, not necessarily in the model.

Let \(a\) be the shared auxiliary variable. First draw \(a\), then draw the unknowns conditionally on it; \(\phi\) collects the parameters of this sampling construction.

Let \(q(z)=\int q(z\mid a)q(a)\,da\), with \(q(z\mid a)\) potentially factorized. Integrating out a shared \(a\) can make the coordinates dependent. Since \(\log q(z)\) is hard to compute, introduce \(r(a\mid z)\) and optimize
\[
\mathbb E_{q(z,a)}[\log \gamma(z)+\log r(a\mid z)-\log q(z,a)].
\]
As in [MCMC–VI bridge](#paper-36), the gap from the marginal ELBO is an expected KL involving the auxiliary conditional. In [MCMC–VI bridge](#paper-36) the auxiliary variables encode a trajectory. In [Hierarchical VI](#paper-37) they index distributions in a mixture.

**Conditional independence can produce marginal dependence.** Suppose \(a\sim\mathcal N(0,\tau^2)\) and, conditional on \(a\), the coordinates are independently distributed as \(z_i\mid a\sim\mathcal N(a,s^2)\). Although the conditional factors are independent,

\[
\operatorname{Cov}(z_i,z_j)
=\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbb E[z_i\mid a],\mathbb E[z_j\mid a])
=\tau^2\quad(i\ne j).
\]

A shared random mean moves them together. The same idea can create more complex dependencies by randomizing other parameters of otherwise simple factors. In the paper's construction, the auxiliary hierarchy is part of the approximation; it need not be a latent variable in the original generative model.

This is useful even for discrete unknowns. For example, random shared Bernoulli probabilities can couple bits without applying an invertible continuous flow directly to those bits. The flexibility lives in the distribution over the factor parameters.

**The new bottleneck is entropy.** Sampling the hierarchy is easy, but integrating it out to compute \(\log q(z)\) can be hard. The reverse distribution \(r(a\mid z)\) makes a bound tractable, at the cost of another approximation to learn. If it poorly identifies which auxiliary settings could have generated a sample, the auxiliary gap can remain large. A richer hierarchy therefore helps only when its flexibility can also be trained effectively.
