Generalized reparameterization tells us what happens when transformed noise is still parameter-dependent. A rejection sampler creates that exact situation for a less obvious reason: among all proposed noise values, only some are accepted, and the selection rule changes with the parameters.

Suppose a proposal is generated as \(z=T_\phi(\epsilon)\) and accepted through a probability depending on \(z\). Among **accepted** draws, the density of \(\epsilon\) is proportional to its proposal density times the acceptance probability. The accepted noise therefore generally depends on \(\phi\), even when the original proposal noise does not. Differentiate through \(T_\phi\) and add the correction from differentiating the accepted-noise density. This is the same decomposition as [Generalized reparameterization](#paper-9), with the transform supplied by a sampler. It explains why simply backpropagating through the accepted proposal can miss part of the derivative.

**The subtlety is selection.** Imagine a parameter change that leaves a proposed value almost unchanged but makes it more likely to be accepted. Differentiating only the proposed value misses that change in which values reach the output. A rejection sampler is not a single smooth path: changing a parameter can change which proposal becomes the accepted one.

The paper avoids differentiating the entire sequence of accept/reject decisions. It instead describes the distribution of the noise *after acceptance*. Once that distribution is known, its derivative supplies the missing selection effect. The resulting estimator combines a derivative through the accepted proposal with a correction for the accepted-noise law.

When the proposal closely matches the variational distribution, acceptance is nearly uniform over proposal draws. The selection effect is then small, which explains why a good rejection sampler can also give a useful gradient estimator. The paper applies this idea to families such as Gamma and Dirichlet, for which practical samplers already use rejection constructions.

**When it becomes costly.** A poor proposal causes many rejections and a stronger correction; both computation and gradient noise can increase. Access to a sampler's output alone is insufficient: this derivation needs its proposal transformation and the quantities defining acceptance. [Implicit gradients](#paper-11) separate the derivative more directly from the sampler implementation.

**What carries forward.** The core idea is to differentiate the *law of accepted randomness*, not an accept/reject branch in a program. This can be reused whenever a proposal sampler has an accessible acceptance mechanism, but not from an opaque sample-only interface.
