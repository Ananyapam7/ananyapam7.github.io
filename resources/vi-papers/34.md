Better gradients cannot make a diagonal Gaussian represent a curved or multimodal posterior. Normalizing flows change a different piece of the problem: the set of distributions available to approximate the target. By transforming a simple draw and tracking the change in density, they retain an evaluable ELBO while fitting richer shapes.

Draw \(z_0\sim q_{\phi,0}\) and set \(z_\ell=T_{\phi,\ell}(z_{\ell-1})\) for invertible maps \(T_{\phi,\ell}\). Here \(\ell=1,\ldots,L\) indexes flow layers, and \(\phi\) collects their parameters. The final density \(q_\phi=q_{\phi,L}\) is

\[
\log q_{\phi,L}(z_L)=\log q_{\phi,0}(z_0)-\sum_{\ell=1}^L\log|\det DT_{\phi,\ell}(z_{\ell-1})|.
\]

Use \(q_{\phi,L}\) in the ordinary ELBO. The new object is a flexible distribution obtained by deforming a simple one, with density evaluation made possible by tractable Jacobian determinants. [AEVB](#paper-4) already uses a transform to differentiate a sample. [Normalizing flows](#paper-34) makes the transform itself part of a richer variational family.

**A transform changes both shape and density height.** In one dimension, \(z_1=2z_0\) doubles the width of every small interval, so the density must halve. The correction is \(-\log2\). In several dimensions, the Jacobian determinant measures the corresponding local volume change. This bookkeeping is what lets a flexible transformed sampler remain usable inside an ELBO that requires \(\log q_\phi\).

A more interesting two-dimensional example is the shear

\[
z_1=u_1,\qquad z_2=u_2+a(u_1^2-1),
\qquad u\sim\mathcal N(0,I).
\]

It bends a circular Gaussian cloud into a curved distribution. Its Jacobian is triangular with determinant one, so the density at the transformed point is simply the original density at \(u\). The coordinates become dependent even though the base coordinates were independent.

Stacking transformations lets small, tractable deformations build more complicated geometry. The original paper develops planar and radial flows as economical building blocks.

**The constraints are computational.** A general expressive map may have an expensive determinant or fail to be invertible. Restricted layers make density evaluation feasible but may need substantial depth to fit difficult geometry. A continuous invertible transformation of a full-support Gaussian also cannot create a truly disconnected support, though it can form separated high-density regions joined by low density. [IAF](#paper-35) chooses a transformation structure suited to efficient variational sampling.

**What carries forward.** The reusable building block is the change-of-variables identity. A transform used only to *differentiate* a sample is not the same as a learned invertible map used to *enlarge* the variational family.
