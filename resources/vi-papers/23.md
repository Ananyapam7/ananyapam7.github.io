Pathwise gradients use local derivatives of the model, but those derivatives can still fluctuate substantially between samples. A tractable approximation to their variation can serve as a control variate: subtract its sampled value and add back its known expectation.

With a reparameterized Gaussian approximation, the paper builds a tractable gradient control from a Taylor expansion of the log joint's derivative. The sampled residual plus the control's exact expectation still estimates the original ELBO gradient. It approximates the fluctuations to subtract; it does not replace the posterior target by a quadratic model.

Use the pathwise gradient of a Taylor approximation as a correlated random quantity \(C\). Then use \(\widehat g_\phi-C+\mathbb E[C]\), where \(\mathbb E[C]\) is available from known moments of the base noise. The estimator is accurate when the random part of the Taylor gradient tracks the random part of the true gradient. This is the same subtract-and-add-back logic as MuProp, now applied to continuous pathwise gradients. Unlike [Sticking the Landing](#paper-22), the paper constructs a surrogate for the varying integrand.

**Predict the fluctuation, then average that prediction exactly.** For a scalar Gaussian draw \(z=m+s\epsilon\), the mean-gradient contribution of \(f\) is \(f'(z)\). A local approximation gives

\[
f'(m+s\epsilon)\approx f'(m)+f''(m)s\epsilon.
\]

The second term explains how a draw above or below the mean changes the gradient, and its expectation is zero. Subtracting it leaves the residual from curvature beyond the local approximation. If \(f\) is quadratic, \(f'\) is exactly linear, so this correction makes the mean-gradient deterministic.

The paper extends this reasoning to the parameters of a Gaussian approximation, using curvature information and tractable noise moments. It is closely related to regression: a useful control is correlated with the gradient, so the variation it explains can be removed without changing the average update.

**When the extra work pays off.** Smooth targets that look approximately quadratic over the region occupied by \(q_\phi\) are favorable. If the approximation spans highly nonlinear regions or separated modes, a tangent near its mean may predict little of the observed variation. Curvature computations also have a cost, even with Hessian–vector products. [Learned quadratic controls](#paper-25) replaces a pointwise Taylor choice with a surrogate trained to explain the gradient variation across sampled points.
