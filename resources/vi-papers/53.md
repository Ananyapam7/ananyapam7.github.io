The [CAVI](#paper-1) formula can be correct yet unusable: exponentiating an expected log joint need not produce a density we know how to normalize. Wang and Blei address this obstacle with local quadratic approximations for continuous nonconjugate variables.

For one such block \(z_j\), define

\[
f_j(z_j)=\mathbb E_{q_{-j}}[\log\gamma(z)].
\]

The ideal update is proportional to \(\exp f_j\). **Laplace variational inference** finds a local maximizer \(m_j\) of \(f_j\) and uses its curvature:

\[
q_j(z_j)\approx\mathcal N(z_j\mid m_j,\Sigma_j),
\qquad
\Sigma_j=[-\nabla^2f_j(m_j)]^{-1}.
\]

A quadratic log density is Gaussian. The Hessian tells us how quickly the expected log joint falls away from its maximum: steep directions get smaller variances. Other factors are updated using expectations under this Gaussian, and the process repeats.

**Delta-method VI approximates the expectation instead.** For a Gaussian block,

\[
\mathbb E_{q_j}[f_j(z_j)]
\approx f_j(m_j)+\tfrac12\operatorname{tr}
\!\left(\nabla^2f_j(m_j)\Sigma_j\right).
\]

Adding the Gaussian entropy gives a tractable approximate objective to optimize over \(m_j,\Sigma_j\). The distinction is where the Taylor expansion enters: a coordinate density in Laplace VI, or an expected objective in delta-method VI.

For intuition, consider \(f_j(z_j)=-a(z_j-b)^2/2+c\), with \(a>0\). Both approaches recover a Gaussian centered at \(b\) with variance \(1/a\). If the actual log density bends differently away from \(b\), the same quadratic becomes a local description whose quality depends on how much probability lies beyond that neighborhood.

**What changes.** Mean-field factorization remains; difficult coordinate calculations are approximated. The paper develops this within models combining smooth nonconjugate blocks and tractable exponential-family structure. It is useful for problems such as Bayesian logistic regression, but a local Gaussian can miss skewness, heavy tails, or separated modes. Approximate updates need not increase the original ELBO.

[Stochastic search](#paper-66) offers a contrasting response: estimate the missing calculation and correct its bias rather than optimize a Taylor approximation.
