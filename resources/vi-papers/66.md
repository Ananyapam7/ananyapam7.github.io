When an ELBO contains an intractable expectation, one response is to replace it with a tractable bound. Paisley and colleagues instead use that approximation to make an unbiased stochastic gradient less noisy.

Suppose the difficult term is \(\mathbb E_{q_\phi}[f(z)]\). Find a correlated surrogate \(h(z)\) whose expectation and derivative are tractable. With its parameters and coefficient \(a\) fixed during the gradient calculation,

\[
\nabla_\phi\mathbb E_{q_\phi}[f]
=a\nabla_\phi\mathbb E_{q_\phi}[h]
+\mathbb E_{q_\phi}[(f-ah)S_\phi],
\qquad S_\phi=\nabla_\phi\log q_\phi.
\]

The first term is computed analytically. Monte Carlo estimates only the residual term.

**Use the approximation as a prediction, then correct its error.** If \(h\) tracks the variation in \(f\), then \(f-ah\) fluctuates less than \(f\). The analytically integrated part carries most of the gradient, while samples supply what it missed.

For example, write a smooth scalar function around \(b\) as

\[
f(z)=\underbrace{f(b)+f'(b)(z-b)+
\tfrac12f''(b)(z-b)^2}_{h(z)}
+r(z).
\]

Under a Gaussian approximation, the quadratic expectation is analytic. Sampling \(r(z)S_\phi(z)\) corrects the Taylor approximation. If \(f\) is exactly quadratic and \(a=1\), the residual vanishes.

**What changes.** The original ELBO is retained. Unlike [delta-method VI](#paper-53), the surrogate does not become the final optimization objective. It need not even be a lower bound; correlation and tractable expectations are what matter.

This is especially useful for nonconjugate terms embedded in an otherwise tractable model. The paper develops stochastic search with control variates and adapts sample effort to gradient noise. It is a direct conceptual companion to [BBVI](#paper-3) and later [approximation-based controls](#paper-25).

A poor surrogate can leave substantial residual noise, and fitting the control adds overhead. Coefficients estimated from the same draws used for the final gradient can introduce bias, so pilot estimates or an appropriate correction are needed. Preserving the expected gradient removes approximation bias from this calculation; it does not remove restrictions of the variational family.
