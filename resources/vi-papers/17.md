A smooth relaxation is a useful predictor of a discrete cost, but optimizing the relaxation directly changes the question. REBAR combines the two ideas we've just encountered: use the relaxation as a sample-dependent control, then correct its mean contribution so that the estimator still differentiates the original discrete objective.

Let \(u\) be a continuous variable from which a hard transformation produces the discrete sample \(z\). Draw \(\widetilde u\) from the conditional law of \(u\) given \(z\). For a differentiable relaxed function \(c\), the estimator has the form

\[
[f(z)-c(\widetilde u)]\,\nabla_\phi\log q_\phi(z)
+\nabla_\phi c(u)-\nabla_\phi c(\widetilde u),
\]

where the last two derivatives follow the unconditional and conditional sampling transforms, respectively, with \(z\) held fixed in the conditional derivative. Why does this work? Decompose \(\mathbb E[c(u)]\) by conditioning on \(z\), then differentiate it in two ways. The difference of the two pathwise terms cancels the expected baseline-score term. [Concrete](#paper-13) supplies the relaxation. REBAR supplies the correction that preserves the discrete gradient.

**Use a soft version as a predictor, not as the problem to solve.** If the relaxed value closely tracks the discrete function value, \(f(z)-c(\widetilde u)\) fluctuates much less than \(f(z)\). That makes the score term quieter. But because this predictor depends on the discrete sample, it is no longer an ordinary constant baseline. The two pathwise terms pay back exactly the mean that was subtracted.

The conditional draw is essential. It produces a soft sample consistent with the hard choice already made. For a binary threshold, the underlying uniform must be drawn within the interval that produces that outcome. This couples the soft and hard computations so that their fluctuations track one another.

The important change from [Concrete](#paper-13) is therefore where the relaxation is used: REBAR still estimates the gradient of the discrete objective. Its temperature affects variance, rather than introducing relaxation bias into that objective.

**The cost of this correction.** We need unconditional and conditional relaxed samples, additional function evaluations, and derivatives through the relaxed computations. A poor surrogate or an extreme temperature can leave a noisy residual or large pathwise corrections. The method is attractive when the relaxation predicts the discrete function well enough to justify those extra computations. [RELAX](#paper-18) learns a more adaptable predictor.

**What carries forward.** The reusable contribution is an unbiased *conditional-relaxation control variate*. The relaxed function need not define the target loss; it can be used solely to cancel noise. The price is conditional sampling and extra derivatives.
