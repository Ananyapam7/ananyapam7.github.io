Semi-amortized VI can correct a prediction, but sometimes the encoder lacks the information needed to choose the correct local factor at all. An observation alone may not tell us its hidden state when neighboring observations matter. This paper asks which model-dependence structures permit an exact shared inference rule and when more context is required.

For shared inference to attain the same optimum as freely chosen local factors, the optimal local parameters must be expressible as a function of the information supplied to the inference network. If the network sees only \(x_i\) and optimal local parameters require information from other observations, increasing network capacity alone leaves that issue unresolved. The paper characterizes when the gap can close and studies what happens when the inference function is allowed additional inputs. [Amortization gap](#paper-39) diagnoses the gap after fitting. [When amortization works](#paper-41) asks when the model structure makes the gap avoidable in principle.

**Some missing information cannot be supplied by a larger network.** Consider a hidden-state sequence. The same observed value \(x_i\) can appear in two positions, but neighboring observations may support very different states at those positions. A deterministic encoder seeing only \(x_i\) must return the same parameters both times. If the optimal local factors differ, no increase in its width or depth can make that restricted input sufficient.

The paper returns to the CAVI equation,

\[
\log q_i^\star(z_i)
=\mathbb E_{q_{-i}^\star}[\log\gamma(z)]+\text{constant},
\]

and asks which observations can affect its right-hand side. This connects the existence of an ideal inference function to the graphical structure of the model. In a simple hierarchical model, shared global information can be absorbed into the fitted mapping, leaving a suitable local dependence on \(x_i\). Other graphs require additional context; the paper analyzes examples with neighboring observations and denser dependencies.

**The design lesson.** Before enlarging an encoder, check whether it receives the information needed to distinguish the local inference problems. Sequence context, dataset summaries, or other model-appropriate inputs may matter more than capacity. Existence of an ideal function does not guarantee that training finds it, and a fitted mapping for one dataset need not automatically generalize when its global context changes. The structural question comes before the optimization question.

**What carries forward.** Before increasing encoder capacity, ask an information question: are its inputs sufficient to distinguish the needed local solutions? This is a structural limitation, not merely an optimization or universal-approximation issue.
