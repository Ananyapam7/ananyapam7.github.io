We have seen many ways to lower gradient variance empirically. To prove optimization rates, however, we need quantitative control of the noise as parameters and dimension change. This paper bounds reparameterization-gradient second moments using smoothness of the target and moments of the base noise.

For a location-scale sample \(z=m+B\epsilon\), the sampled gradient of a smooth function \(f(z)\) with respect to \(B\) is \(\nabla_z f(z)\epsilon^\top\). Its squared norm contains both \(\|\nabla_z f(z)\|^2\) and \(\|\epsilon\|^2\). If \(f\) has a gradient that changes no faster than \(\beta\) times the change in its input (called \(\beta\)-smoothness), and \(\bar z\) is a stationary point, then \(\|\nabla_z f(z)\|\leq \beta\|z-\bar z\|\). Combining this with standardized base-noise moments bounds the gradient second moment in terms of \((d+1)\|m-\bar z\|^2\) and \((d+\kappa)\|B\|_F^2\), scaled by \(\beta^2\), with \(\kappa\) the base fourth moment in the paper’s setup. The new contribution is a quantitative gradient-noise bound. [BBVI convergence](#paper-45) turns bounds of this type into optimization results.

**Why the scale gradient can be noisier than the mean gradient.** Moving \(m\) shifts every draw equally, so its sampled derivative is \(\nabla_z f(z)\). Changing \(B\) changes the direction and size of each noise realization, producing the outer product \(\nabla_z f(z)\epsilon^\top\). Large noise affects both the location at which the function is evaluated and the multiplier in its gradient.

For the scalar quadratic \(f(z)=\tfrac12 z^2\), take \(m=0\), \(z=s\epsilon\), and standard Gaussian noise. The scale-gradient estimate is \(s\epsilon^2\), giving

\[
\mathbb E[\widehat g_s]=s,
\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(\widehat g_s)=2s^2,
\qquad
\mathbb E[\widehat g_s^2]=3s^2.
\]

The noise grows with the scale being optimized. A global assumption that gradient variance is bounded by one fixed constant would conceal this dependence. The paper instead relates second moments to the location and spread of the approximation, with explicit dimension and base-noise moment factors.

**What the bound tells us.** Smoothness limits how quickly the function's gradient can grow away from a stationary point; the base fourth moment controls how often noise produces large products. Heavy tails or an unbounded Hessian can break this particular argument. The result supports optimization theory rather than proving that every practical model has low variance. [Convergence analysis](#paper-45) uses this parameter-dependent view of noise.

**What carries forward.** Its reusable lesson is that a *parameter-dependent* variance bound can be more faithful than assuming uniform noise everywhere. The results are estimator guarantees under smoothness and moment assumptions, not claims about posterior accuracy.
