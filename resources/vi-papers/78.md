**The obstacle.** Hierarchical mixtures are flexible and easy to sample, but the marginal density in the ELBO is often intractable.

**Mathematical move.** Draw auxiliary parameters \(u\sim r_\phi\), then \(z\sim q_\phi(z\mid u)\), producing \(q_\phi(z)=\int q_\phi(z\mid u)r_\phi(u)\,du\). Replace the difficult marginal \(\log q_\phi(z)\) by a finite-mixture expression built from independent auxiliary draws, e.g. \(\log\frac1{K+1}\sum_{k=0}^K q_\phi(z\mid u_k)\), where \(u_0\) generated \(z\). This leads to surrogate bounds that become tight as the auxiliary sample size increases under suitable conditions.

**What changes.** The family **and** its tractable optimization surrogate. Sampling ability no longer implies density evaluability; SIVI bridges that gap with finite auxiliary mixtures.

More explicitly, draw \(u_0,\ldots,u_K\) independently from \(r_\phi\), and then draw \(z\) from \(q_\phi(z\mid u_0)\). The lower bound is
\[
\mathcal L_K=\mathbb E\!\left[\log\gamma(z)-\log\left\{\frac1{K+1}\sum_{k=0}^K q_\phi(z\mid u_k)\right\}\right]
\leq\mathcal L(\phi).
\]
Including the component that generated the sample is essential to this construction. Increasing \(K\) improves the surrogate but costs more conditional-density evaluations. Unlike adversarial VI, this route does not require training a density-ratio discriminator. Small auxiliary samples can leave a loose training objective even when the family is expressive.
