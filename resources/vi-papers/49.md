Many pathwise control variates require moments of the variational distribution in closed form. A complicated transformation, such as a flow, may make those moments unavailable even though sampling is easy.

This paper applies zero-variance controls based on integration by parts to pathwise VI gradients. Working with the base noise makes controls available even when the transformed approximation lacks tractable moments, as can happen with flows. It also compares existing controls and coefficient-fitting approaches. The model, family, and ELBO stay fixed; the addition is a variance-reduction mechanism whose usefulness depends on its computational cost.

Choose a smooth scalar function \(P\) to build a quantity whose expectation is zero. The Langevin Stein construction is
\[
C_P(z)=\Delta P(z)+\nabla P(z)^\top\nabla\log q(z).
\]
Here \(\Delta P\) is the sum of the second derivatives of \(P\), and \(\nabla\log q\) is the density score with respect to its input. Its expectation under \(q\) vanishes under suitable boundary conditions because \(qC_P=\nabla\cdot(q\nabla P)\). The paper applies this construction to pathwise gradients, focusing on linear \(P\), which gives controls made from score components. For standard Gaussian base noise those components are \(-\epsilon_i\). [Learned quadratic controls](#paper-25) gets the exact mean of a control from moments of a quadratic surrogate. This paper gets it from integration by parts and fits coefficients to correlate the controls with the gradient.

**Build controls whose average is known without approximating the model.** With standard Gaussian base noise, its score is \(-\epsilon\). Every component has mean zero. If a scalar gradient estimate behaves approximately like \(a+b^\top\epsilon\), a suitable linear combination of these controls can remove the \(b^\top\epsilon\) fluctuation while leaving \(a\) untouched.

For example, a Gaussian mean-gradient contribution \(m+s\epsilon\) can be made deterministic by subtracting \(s\epsilon\). A scale-gradient contribution \(s\epsilon^2\), however, is uncorrelated with a linear control under symmetric Gaussian noise, since \(\mathbb E[\epsilon^3]=0\). The same controls that perfectly explain one component may do little for another. This gives a concrete reason why the construction's usefulness depends on gradient structure.

The paper fits control coefficients using a least-squares formulation and focuses on first-order polynomial Stein controls. Integration by parts supplies their zero mean; the fitting procedure tries to align them with the random variation of each gradient component. For pathwise estimators, constructing the controls in base-noise space keeps them particularly simple.

**The practical tradeoff.** More coefficients require more information to fit reliably, and poor correlation leaves little variance to remove. Boundary behavior matters for the zero-mean identity outside familiar Gaussian settings. Coefficient fitting also needs care if the same draws are reused for evaluation. The method offers inexpensive controls, not a guarantee that a first-order basis captures every important source of noise.
