The ELBO averages a log importance weight. An alternative is to average several importance weights first and then take the logarithm, giving a different training criterion from the same proposal distribution.

IWAE keeps the latent-variable generative model and an amortized proposal \(q_\phi(z\mid x)\), but replaces the single-sample ELBO with a log average of importance weights from \(K\) draws. The primary change is the training bound. Multiple weighted samples can yield a richer effective approximation, but they do not automatically enlarge the underlying parametric family of the encoder.

For independent draws \(z^{(s)}\sim q_\phi\), write \(w_s=w_\phi(z^{(s)})\). Replace \(\mathbb E[\log w]\) with
\[
\mathcal L_K=\mathbb E_{q_\phi^{\otimes K}}\!\left[\log\left(\frac1K\sum_{s=1}^K w_s\right)\right].
\]
The average weight estimates \(Z\), so Jensen’s inequality gives \(\mathcal L_K\leq\log Z\). The bounds increase with \(K\) under the paper’s conditions. The important distinction is the **position of the logarithm**. Averaging \(K\) independent estimates of \(\log w\) estimates the original ELBO more precisely. Taking the log *after* averaging weights targets a different objective. [VIMCO](#paper-19) and [DReG](#paper-33) estimate derivatives of this kind of multisample objective.

**A set of samples gets judged together.** Suppose two weights are \(1\) and \(9\). Averaging their log weights gives \(\tfrac12(\log1+\log9)=\log3\). Taking the log of their average gives \(\log5\). The second construction lets the large weight compensate for the small one within the set. This is the change in objective, not merely a less noisy estimate of the first number.

Differentiating the sampled objective shows how credit is allocated:

\[
\nabla\log\!\left(\frac1K\sum_s w_s\right)
=\sum_s\bar w_s\nabla\log w_s.
\]

Samples with larger importance weights receive more influence. The encoder is trained through sets of possible explanations of an observation, and the paper finds that this can support richer use of latent variables than the single-sample objective.

**Why more samples are not a complete solution.** If the proposal rarely visits an important region, many draws can still miss it. If one weight dominates, the effective contribution of the other samples is small. And although the objective becomes a tighter bound, it also becomes less sensitive to the inference parameters as it approaches \(\log Z\). [Tighter is not always better](#paper-32) separates these two effects; [DReG](#paper-33) improves the inference-gradient estimator. A larger \(K\) changes both the statistical objective and the computation needed to train it.
