An optimizer may find the best mean-field distribution for a fixed dataset. But does that approximation learn the true underlying parameter as the dataset grows? The consistency question concerns statistical behavior across datasets, not whether a numerical algorithm converges on one dataset.

The global unknown is a block \(z_g\) of \(z\); the question is what happens to its variational posterior as the dataset grows.

Profile out the local variational factors to obtain a variational criterion for the global parameter. Under regularity and local asymptotic normality conditions on this criterion, the appropriately rescaled VB posterior approaches the KL projection of a limiting Gaussian onto the variational family. There are two distinct claims: the unscaled approximation can concentrate at the true parameter, while its local, rescaled covariance can still differ from the limiting Gaussian’s covariance. This is a statistical result as the amount of data grows. [Optimization guarantees](#vi-guarantees) instead ask how closely an algorithm can approach the variational optimum for a given dataset.

**Getting the center right is different from getting uncertainty right.** Suppose a two-dimensional posterior becomes approximately Gaussian with covariance

\[
\Sigma_N=\frac1N
\begin{pmatrix}1&\rho\\\rho&1\end{pmatrix}.
\]

Its reverse-KL projection onto diagonal Gaussians has the same mean, but each variance is \((1-\rho^2)/N\). Both the true posterior and the projection concentrate as \(N\) grows. Yet their ratio of variances remains \(1-\rho^2\), so more data does not remove the relative underestimation of uncertainty. This is the local Gaussian version of the [mean-field geometry](#method-mfvi).

The paper establishes a variational Bernstein–von Mises theory under assumptions on the profiled variational criterion. After centering and rescaling, the variational posterior behaves like a KL projection of a limiting normal distribution. This gives a route to consistency and asymptotic descriptions, rather than assuming that the computational approximation becomes exact with enough data.

**What the theorem depends on.** The variational criterion must have the appropriate local asymptotic behavior and concentrate around the relevant parameter. Poor identification, an inconsistent profiled criterion, or other failures of these conditions can invalidate the conclusion. The theory concerns the variational solution itself; an algorithm trapped at a poor local solution is a separate issue. For uncertainty correction at a fitted solution, [linear response](#paper-43) takes a different route.

**What carries forward.** The reusable distinction is *consistency versus uncertainty calibration*. A variational posterior can concentrate at the right point while retaining the wrong rescaled covariance; optimization convergence is a third, separate question.
