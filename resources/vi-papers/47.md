Sticking the Landing offered a remarkable estimator property: at an exact fit, every sampled gradient can vanish. This paper asks what that implies for the *whole optimization trajectory*. If noise shrinks at the same time as the remaining optimization error, geometric convergence becomes possible under suitable assumptions.

[Sticking the Landing](#paper-22) shows pointwise cancellation at an exact fit. This paper proves a bound that controls STL gradient noise as the parameters approach the solution. Under perfect variational-family specification and the paper’s smoothness and convexity conditions, the variance bound vanishes quadratically with distance to the optimum, allowing geometric convergence. Under misspecification, a residual term can remain. The new result is the link between an estimator’s variance *near* the solution and the optimizer’s convergence rate.

**An optimizer behaves differently when its noise disappears at the solution.** With persistent gradient noise, a fixed step size tends to keep pushing the parameters around even after they approach the optimum. Decreasing the step size suppresses those pushes but also slows progress. If the noise shrinks with the remaining error, the algorithm can keep taking effective steps without the same noise floor.

A schematic form of the paper's bound is

\[
\mathbb E[\|\widehat g_\phi\|^2]
\leq A\|\phi-\phi^\star\|^2+B_{\mathrm{mis}},
\]

where the residual term reflects mismatch between the variational family and target. Under perfect specification, STL's cancellation removes this residual. Together with the required strong convexity and smoothness, this supports geometric contraction of the optimization error.

**A correlated Gaussian explains the limitation.** A full-rank Gaussian family can match a Gaussian posterior exactly. A diagonal family cannot match a strongly correlated Gaussian, so spatial log-density gradients still disagree at its best fit. STL then retains a random component. The paper quantifies misspecification through a score-discrepancy quantity and shows settings where STL can be worse than an estimator using closed-form entropy.

Thus this result gives a reason to expect particularly fast optimization when the family is well matched, not an unconditional preference for STL. The estimator, the family, and the parameterization jointly determine whether the noise vanishes where the optimizer is trying to go.

**What carries forward.** The general lesson is that the behavior of gradient noise *near the solution* matters, not only its variance at initialization. Misspecification may remove the vanishing-noise advantage.
