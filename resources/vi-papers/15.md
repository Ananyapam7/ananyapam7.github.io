Concrete gives smooth gradients by changing the sampled object. But what if we want the original discrete objective unchanged? NVIL returns to the score estimator: rather than smoothing the discrete choice, it subtracts a baseline predicting the part of its learning signal that is already explained by the observation.

NVIL uses a score estimator of the form \((\log w-b(x))\nabla_\phi\log q_\phi(z\mid x)\), with a learned input-dependent baseline and other variance-reduction measures. For fixed \(x\), the conditional expectation of the score is zero, so subtracting \(b(x)\) preserves the expected gradient. [BBVI](#paper-3) already uses the score as a control variate. NVIL makes the baseline depend on the observation whose discrete latent variables are being inferred.

**Credit should be relative to the observation.** A difficult observation may have a low log weight for almost every latent configuration, while an easy one has a high log weight. That difference can dominate the score estimator even though the useful question is which latent configuration explains *this* observation better. An input-dependent baseline learns the predictable part of the learning signal before seeing the random latent draw.

For a Bernoulli latent variable with probability \(p=\operatorname{sigmoid}(\phi)\), the score is \(z-p\). The update is proportional to \((\log w-b(x))(z-p)\): a better-than-baseline draw encourages the probability of the sampled choice, while a worse one discourages it. Centering the signal makes that comparison easier to estimate.

NVIL also uses variance normalization and more local learning signals for multilayer models. These choices turn a very general but noisy score identity into a practical way to train discrete belief networks with an inference network.

**What a baseline cannot explain.** It can predict difficulty from \(x\), but it cannot distinguish two draws for the same \(x\) if it never sees \(z\). Strong variation between latent configurations remains. Giving a baseline arbitrary dependence on the draw would lose the simple zero-mean argument; [MuProp](#paper-16) and [REBAR](#paper-17) introduce the corrections needed to use richer, sample-dependent controls.

**What carries forward.** An input-dependent baseline is broadly reusable whenever the score has conditional mean zero. It reduces avoidable variation without altering the expected gradient, but cannot by itself explain differences among latent draws for the same observation.
