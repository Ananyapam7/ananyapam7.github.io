All the previous pathwise arguments rely on draws that can move smoothly. A categorical value cannot: a tiny parameter change usually leaves the sampled category unchanged, then occasionally flips it. The Concrete distribution creates a continuously moving substitute, so differentiation is possible—but now we must ask whether we're differentiating the original problem or a relaxed one.

A categorical draw can be generated by taking \(\arg\max_i(\ell_i+\epsilon_i)\), where \(\ell_i\) are logits (unnormalized log probabilities) and \(\epsilon_i\) are independent Gumbel draws. Replace the hard maximum with

\[
y_i=\operatorname{softmax}\!\big((\ell+\epsilon)/\tau\big)_i.
\]

For \(\tau\gt 0\), \(y\) lies in the simplex and is differentiable in \(\ell\). As \(\tau\downarrow0\), it approaches the one-hot draw. Differentiating the relaxed objective generally gives a biased gradient for the original discrete objective. [REBAR](#paper-17) keeps the discrete objective and uses the relaxation inside a control variate.

**Why soften a choice?** A hard categorical sample stays on one vertex until a logit crosses a threshold; its derivative is zero almost everywhere. The relaxed sample moves through the simplex's interior. For three categories, a point such as \((0.8,0.15,0.05)\) expresses a soft choice that can move continuously toward a different vertex, allowing a downstream differentiable function to send a gradient back to the logits.

The temperature controls that movement. A high temperature gives smooth but less discrete samples. A low temperature places samples close to vertices, but the derivative

\[
\frac{\partial y_i}{\partial\ell_j}
=\frac1\tau y_i(\mathbf1\{i=j\}-y_j)
\]

reveals the difficulty: it is tiny for decisive choices and can be very large near a tie. Taking the temperature toward zero does not give an effortless discrete gradient.

**When the relaxation is useful.** The downstream computation must make sense on soft inputs. If fractional categories behave very differently from every actual category, the relaxed optimizer may exploit those interior states, creating a gap when we return to hard choices. Temperature selection trades this mismatch against gradient quality. [REBAR](#paper-17) uses the same smoothness to reduce variance while correcting back to the original discrete objective.

**What carries forward.** A relaxation can be used either as a *new surrogate objective* or as a *component of an unbiased estimator*. Concrete follows the first route; REBAR later illustrates the second. Keeping that distinction visible prevents a common confusion.
