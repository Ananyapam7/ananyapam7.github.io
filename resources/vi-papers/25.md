A Taylor control uses derivatives at a chosen expansion point. That point may be unrepresentative of a broad variational distribution, and calculating curvature can be expensive. Instead, this paper fits a quadratic surrogate specifically to explain the gradient variation in the region actually visited by the approximation.

Use a fitted quadratic \(\tilde f(z)=a+b^\top z+\tfrac12 z^\top Hz\). Its expected value depends on \(\mathbb E_q[z]\) and \(\mathbb E_q[zz^\top]\), so the surrogate gradient can be averaged analytically when these moments are known and tractable to differentiate. Subtract the sampled surrogate gradient and add back its exact expectation. The paper learns the quadratic to reduce **gradient variance**. That is the difference from simply deriving the quadratic from the model’s Taylor expansion at a point, as in [Taylor controls](#paper-23).

**A quadratic is useful because its average is easy.** For a Gaussian with mean \(m\) and covariance \(\Sigma\),

\[
\mathbb E_q[\tilde f(z)]
=a+b^\top m+\tfrac12m^\top Hm
+\tfrac12\operatorname{tr}(H\Sigma).
\]

We can differentiate this expression exactly. The expensive function \(f\) is still evaluated on a draw, but the quadratic's sampled gradient is subtracted and its analytic gradient added back. Thus the approximation does not replace the target: errors in the quadratic affect variance rather than changing the intended expectation.

The important modeling choice is what to fit. A Taylor expansion matches derivatives at one point. This paper instead fits a quadratic using a variance objective, or a cheaper gradient-matching proxy, across the region explored by the current approximation. Low-rank structure makes the surrogate practical when a dense quadratic would be expensive.

**Where it helps and where it stalls.** A broad region with predictable curvature can be a good match even if a tangent at the mean is inadequate. Strongly nonquadratic gradients can leave a large residual, while fitting a high-rank surrogate can cost too much. As \(q_\phi\) moves, the surrogate must keep up. [Joint controls](#paper-26) adds a further concern: even an excellent noise control for each observation may leave substantial variability across observations.

**What carries forward.** What transfers is the distinction between *approximating a function accurately* and *approximating the noise in its derivative*. The latter is what a control variate needs, provided the surrogate's expectation remains tractable.
