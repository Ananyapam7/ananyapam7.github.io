Variational inference and Foundations

Start with an observation. Build a target. Fit a distribution.

Prerequisites: probability densities, expectations, and basic calculus.

In Bayesian inference, we start with observed data, \(x\), and quantities we do not know but want to learn about, \(z\). These unknowns might be regression coefficients, missing labels, hidden states, or an entire function. How should observing \(x\) change what we think is plausible about \(z\)?

What is unknown, and what is a model?

Imagine measuring a quantity with a noisy instrument. We see the readings, but not the underlying value without measurement error. Different underlying values could plausibly have produced the same readings. Reporting just one guess would hide that uncertainty.

A statistical model specifies a collection of possible probability distributions for the observations. A Bayesian model also describes uncertainty about the unknown quantities, giving a joint probability model for the observations and unknowns. This makes our assumptions explicit: how readings vary around an underlying value, for example, and whether measurement errors are independent.

A probability distribution lets us assign different amounts of plausibility to different possibilities and calculate the probability that an unknown lies in a given range. Treating an unknown parameter probabilistically does not mean its physical value must fluctuate; the distribution represents uncertainty about it. For a continuous quantity, probabilities are areas under a density, rather than the height of the density at a single point.

For now, we take the model as given. We use \(z\) for the quantities whose posterior we want to approximate.

For example, \(z\) might contain regression coefficients we want to infer, while \(\theta\) specifies a known measurement-noise level.

How a Bayesian model turns observations into an update

We need two ingredients. A prior distribution, \(p_\theta(z)\), describes uncertainty about \(z\) before conditioning on the observed data. A sampling distribution, \(p_\theta(x\mid z)\), describes how observations could vary for a given \(z\), under the fixed settings \(\theta\).

Before observing data, the sampling distribution is a distribution over possible observations. After we observe \(x\), we hold it fixed and view the same expression as a function of \(z\). In that role, it is called the likelihood. A likelihood is not generally a probability distribution over the unknown quantities.

Return to the noisy instrument. For each possible true value, ask how compatible it is with the reading we observed. The likelihood gives more weight to values that make that reading plausible and less weight to values that make it surprising. Multiplying by the prior reweights our initial uncertainty using this compatibility with the data. Normalization then turns the resulting weights into a probability distribution.

Those two ingredients specify one joint distribution. The probability product rule lets us write it in two ways:

\[ p_\theta(x,z) = \underbrace{p_\theta(z)\,p_\theta(x\mid z)}_{\text{prior and sampling model}} = \underbrace{p_\theta(x)\,p_\theta(z\mid x)}_{\text{evidence and posterior}}. \]

Rearranging gives Bayes’ rule. The posterior \(p_\theta(z\mid x)\) is the updated distribution over \(z\), obtained by multiplying the prior by the likelihood and dividing by the evidence \(p_\theta(x)\).

For the interactive example, let the prior be \(z\sim\mathcal N(0,1)\) and the observation model be \(x\mid z\sim\mathcal N(z,0.8^2)\). Completing the square gives

\[z\mid x\sim\mathcal N\!\left(\frac{x}{1+0.8^2},\frac{0.8^2}{1+0.8^2}\right).\]

The posterior mean lies between the prior mean and the observation. Its variance is smaller than the prior variance. Moving the observation changes the center; the fixed observation noise keeps the posterior spread constant.

Figure 1. The prior and likelihood are functions of the same \(z\). Multiply them pointwise, then rescale the product to have area one. In this illustration, the posterior shifts toward the values favored by the data.

The joint notation works across models because it leaves open what’s inside each factor. In regression, \(z\) can be a vector of coefficients and we can condition on known inputs. In a state-space model, \(z\) can be a trajectory whose successive states depend on each other. In a mixture model, \(z\) can contain both unknown group labels and continuous group parameters. The update is the same. Only the assumptions that specify the joint probability are different.

From Bayesian inference to a more general inference problem

We have seen that Bayesian inference gives us a posterior distribution over the unknown quantities \(z\), conditional on the observed data \(x\). For continuous \(z\), Bayes’ rule gives

\[ p_\theta(z\mid x)=\frac{p_\theta(x,z)}{\int p_\theta(x,z')\,dz'}. \]

At first glance, this seems to solve our problem: we have an expression for the posterior we wanted. But writing down a distribution and being able to compute or work with it are different things.

In many models, evaluating the joint density \(p_\theta(x,z)\) at a particular value of \(z\) is straightforward. The denominator requires integrating over every possible value. For simple models, this integral may be available analytically. For more complicated models, particularly when the unknowns are high-dimensional and dependent, it can be computationally intractable.

We therefore often know the posterior only up to a proportionality constant:

\[ p_\theta(z\mid x)\propto p_\theta(x,z). \]

This suggests a more general formulation. Suppose we are given a nonnegative function \(\gamma(z)\), and we want to work with the probability distribution

\[ \boxed{\pi(z)=\frac{\gamma(z)}{Z},\qquad Z=\int\gamma(z)\,dz.} \]

We assume \(0\lt Z\lt\infty\), so the target is well defined. Here, \(\gamma\) is the unnormalized density, \(\pi\) is the normalized target density, and \(Z\) is the normalizing constant. For discrete variables, we replace the integral with a sum.

The Bayesian case fits this notation exactly:

\[ \gamma(z)=p_\theta(x,z),\qquad \pi(z)=p_\theta(z\mid x),\qquad Z=p_\theta(x). \]

Computing a Bayesian posterior is thus one instance of the broader problem of working with a probability distribution specified only up to normalization.

Why is this formulation more general?

The function \(\gamma\) need not come from a prior and a likelihood.

In statistical physics, we may start with an energy function \(U(z)\) for a system in state \(z\). Its Boltzmann distribution at temperature \(T>0\), in units where Boltzmann’s constant is one, is

\[ \pi(z)=\frac{\exp[-U(z)/T]}{Z}. \]

Here, \(\gamma(z)=\exp[-U(z)/T]\). We do not need an observed dataset or a Bayesian posterior to define this target.

In an energy-based model, an energy function \(U_\theta(z)\) similarly assigns relative plausibility to configurations:

\[ \pi_\theta(z)=\frac{\exp[-U_\theta(z)]}{Z_\theta}. \]

Lower energy means higher density relative to other configurations.

A Gibbs-type distribution can instead combine a reference density \(p_0(z)\) and a loss function \(R(z)\):

\[ \pi(z)=\frac{p_0(z)\exp[-\lambda R(z)]}{Z},\qquad\lambda>0. \]

Values with smaller loss receive more weight relative to the reference density. The loss need not arise from a likelihood.

These constructions start with different objects: a joint density, an energy function, or a loss. Yet each leads to a target of the form \(\pi\propto\gamma\). This separates defining the probability distribution from performing inference with it.

How can we solve the inference problem?

We can evaluate \(\gamma\), perhaps only up to a multiplicative constant, and want to characterize the corresponding target \(\pi=\gamma/Z\). Depending on the problem, this might mean obtaining its density, drawing samples from it, or constructing a tractable approximation to it. These are different kinds of answers to the same underlying inference problem.

Even knowing \(Z\) does not necessarily make integration, marginalization, or sampling easy. Evaluating a density pointwise and performing inference with it are different tasks; the structure of the target determines which methods are practical.

Why knowing the normalizing constant is not enough

Even if the normalizing constant \(Z\) were known, working with the resulting distribution could still be difficult. Knowing the density at any point is not the same as being able to calculate how much probability lies in a whole region. This distinction becomes particularly important when \(z\) is high-dimensional.

Suppose we know \(Z\) exactly and can evaluate \(\gamma(z)\). Then we can evaluate \(\pi(z)=\gamma(z)/Z\) at any chosen point: the normalization problem is solved. But an event probability and a marginal density still require integration:

\[ \Pr_\pi(z\in B)=\int_B\pi(z)\,dz, \qquad \pi_1(z_1)=\int_{\mathbb R^{d-1}}\pi(z_1,z_2,\ldots,z_d)\,dz_2\cdots dz_d. \]

Here \(B\) is a region of the state space, and \(\pi_1\) is the marginal density of the first coordinate. Computing means and covariances also requires integrals.

A concrete example. Consider the target on \(\mathbb R^d\)

\[ \pi(z)=\frac1Z\exp\!\left(-\frac12 z^\top A z-\lambda\sum_{i=1}^d z_i^4\right), \qquad A=A^\top\succ0,\quad\lambda>0. \]

Suppose someone gives us its exact value. Evaluating the density at a chosen vector \(z\) is now straightforward. Yet the marginal density of \(z_1\) is

\[ \pi_1(z_1)=\frac1Z\int_{\mathbb R^{d-1}} \exp\!\left(-\frac12 z^\top A z-\lambda\sum_{i=1}^d z_i^4\right) \,dz_2\cdots dz_d. \]

For each fixed \(z_1\), this is still a \((d-1)\)-dimensional integral. The off-diagonal entries of \(A\) create cross terms because

\[ z^\top A z=\sum_{i=1}^d A_{ii}z_i^2+2\sum_{i\lt j}A_{ij}z_i z_j. \]

For a general dense \(A\), these terms couple the coordinates, so the integrand does not factor into a product of one-dimensional functions. It gives us the value of one particular integral over the whole space, not all the partial integrals needed for marginals or event probabilities.

Sampling is a separate problem too. Even if a univariate density is normalized and easy to evaluate, generating a random draw requires a procedure. Inverse-transform sampling provides a theoretical construction:

\[ F(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{t}\pi(u)\,du, \qquad z^\star=F^{-1}(U),\quad U\sim\operatorname{Uniform}(0,1). \]

Constructing the CDF already requires integration, and evaluating its inverse may require numerical work. Sampling becomes harder in higher dimensions. This is why rejection sampling and MCMC can be useful even for a normalized target.

Some distributions are easier because of their mathematical form. For \(\pi(z)=\mathcal N(z;\mu,\Sigma)\), with positive-definite \(\Sigma\), the Gaussian structure gives us moments, marginals, and conditionals, as well as a sampling construction: if \(LL^\top=\Sigma\) and \(\varepsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I)\), then \(z^\star=\mu+L\varepsilon\) has the target distribution. This tractability comes from the Gaussian family, not simply from knowing its normalizing constant.

\[ \boxed{\text{Evaluating a density}\;\neq\;\text{performing inference with it}.} \]

The broader problem is therefore how to work with a distribution that may be easy to evaluate pointwise but difficult to integrate, marginalize, or sample from efficiently. Exact methods, Monte Carlo methods, and variational inference address different aspects of this problem.

Exact inference

Some models allow an analytic answer. For example, a Beta prior for a Bernoulli success probability gives a Beta posterior after observing successes and failures. The Gaussian update above is another example: we obtained the posterior mean and variance directly.

Numerical integration and direct sampling

When symbolic calculations are unavailable, numerical integration can approximate the needed integrals. Quadrature can be effective in low dimensions, although the cost of straightforward grid-based methods grows rapidly with dimension.

Alternatively, we may be able to generate independent samples from the target. Rejection sampling is one option if we have a proposal density we can sample from and a suitable bound on the unnormalized target relative to that proposal. It can avoid evaluating \(Z\), but finding a useful bound and achieving a reasonable acceptance rate may be difficult.

Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC)

MCMC constructs a Markov chain with \(\pi\) as its stationary distribution. It does not generally compute \(Z\), provide an explicit normalized density, or produce independent draws.

Target-density ratios cancel the unknown normalizing constant, which lets methods such as Metropolis-Hastings construct transitions without evaluating it. Under suitable convergence conditions, the chain approaches the target distribution. Finite runs can explore slowly, and successive samples are generally correlated.

How do MCMC samples answer questions about the target?

For a function \(f\), the expectation \(\mathbb E_\pi[f(z)]\) is its probability-weighted average. A well-mixing chain lets us estimate it by averaging over its samples:

\[ \mathbb E_\pi[f(z)]\approx\frac1N\sum_{n=1}^N f(z^{(n)}). \]

Choosing \(f(z)=z\) estimates a mean; choosing \(f(z)=\mathbf 1_A(z)\) estimates an event probability; choosing \(f(z)=z^2\) estimates a second moment. The indicator is one inside \(A\) and zero outside it. For scalar \(z\), the variance is \(\mathbb E_\pi[z^2]-(\mathbb E_\pi[z])^2\).

Variational inference

Variational inference (VI) introduces a family of tractable distributions \(q_\phi(z)\), indexed by parameters \(\phi\), and fits a member of that family to the target:

\[ q_{\phi^\star}(z)\approx\pi(z). \]

For example, suppose we allow only Gaussian approximations, \(q_\phi(z)=\mathcal N(z;m,s^2)\), with \(\phi=(m,s)\) and \(s>0\). We can move the center and change the spread, but every candidate remains Gaussian. The parameters \(\phi\) are the controls for those adjustments; fitting the approximation means choosing their settings. This family is tractable because we can evaluate its density and generate samples easily.

A single Gaussian cannot reproduce every target: it cannot match two separated peaks exactly, however carefully we adjust its center and spread. Choosing the family determines which shapes are available; optimization chooses among those shapes.

The result is a fitted distribution that we can use to describe uncertainty and calculate or estimate quantities of interest. Its accuracy depends on both what the family can represent and how well we optimize within it.

What does it mean for one distribution to approximate another well? We need a criterion that compares their probability assignments, and a family in which we can optimize that criterion.

A common choice is reverse Kullback-Leibler divergence:

\[ \mathrm{KL}(q_\phi\|\pi) =\int q_\phi(z)\log\frac{q_\phi(z)}{\pi(z)}\,dz =\mathbb E_{q_\phi}[\log q_\phi(z)-\log\pi(z)]. \]

Imagine drawing values from our current approximation. At each draw, compare the density assigned by the approximation with the density assigned by the target, using their log ratio. Reverse KL averages this comparison over the places the approximation itself visits. Changing the direction of KL changes whose draws we average over.

KL is nonnegative and is zero when the two distributions match. It is not a symmetric distance: exchanging \(q_\phi\) and \(\pi\) changes the criterion.

Why this direction? The expectation is taken under our own approximation, which we choose to make tractable. We can often sample from \(q_\phi\) and evaluate its density even when we cannot directly sample from \(\pi\). The criterion also strongly penalizes putting mass where the target has very little density. Other divergence criteria are possible, and this choice affects which approximation is preferred.

Why can reverse KL favor just one of two peaks?

The two animations show a two-dimensional target with two separated modes. Blue contours stay fixed; the red Gaussian approximation moves and changes shape. Forward KL stretches the red contours across both modes, while reverse KL tightens them around one.

For a precise description, write the target in coordinates \(w=(u,v)\):

\[ \pi_w(u,v)=\left[\tfrac12\mathcal N(u;-3,0.6^2)+\tfrac12\mathcal N(u;3,0.6^2)\right]\mathcal N(v;0,0.8^2), \] \[ q_w(u,v)=\mathcal N(u;m_u,s_u^2)\mathcal N(v;m_v,s_v^2). \]

The plots rotate both distributions by 25 degrees, \(z=Rw\), to place the modes diagonally. This shared rotation changes neither KL objective. Both runs start at \((m_u,m_v)=(1.2,-1)\) and \((s_u,s_v)=(1.3,1.8)\). Only the direction of KL changes:

\[ \underbrace{\mathrm{KL}(\pi\|q)=\mathbb E_\pi[\log\pi-\log q]}_{\text{forward KL: average over the target}}, \qquad \underbrace{\mathrm{KL}(q\|\pi)=\mathbb E_q[\log q-\log\pi]}_{\text{reverse KL: average over the approximation}}. \]

Forward KL asks whether the approximation covers places the target visits. The target visits both peaks. A narrow Gaussian on one peak would assign extremely small density to draws from the other, producing a large penalty. The fitted Gaussian therefore broadens to cover both. For this target, the forward-KL optimum matches its mean and covariance: in the unrotated coordinates, \((m_u,m_v)=(0,0)\) and \((s_u^2,s_v^2)=(9.36,0.64)\). It covers both peaks, but also puts substantial probability in the valley: a single Gaussian cannot reproduce the target’s two-peak shape.

Reverse KL asks whether the places the approximation visits are plausible under the target. A broad Gaussian visits the valley between the peaks, where the target density is tiny. Narrowing around one peak avoids those visits. Once the approximation concentrates there, it rarely visits the other peak, which receives little direct weight in the expectation. Here the run settles near \((m_u,m_v)=(3,0)\) and \((s_u,s_v)=(0.6,0.8)\), matching the upper-right component after rotation.

The target is symmetric, but the starting approximation is offset toward the positive-u mode. That offset selects the upper-right solution in this run; reflecting the initial u-coordinate would select the other mode. The available shapes matter too: a family with two Gaussian components can represent this particular target exactly.

The loss plots show improvement within each run. Their numerical values measure different objectives and should not be compared as a single ranking of the two fits.

With a chosen family and criterion, we seek

\[ \phi^\star\in\operatorname*{arg\,min}_\phi\mathrm{KL}(q_\phi\|\pi). \]

In practice, optimization may find a local optimum.

There is an apparent obstacle: the objective contains \(\pi\), whose normalizing constant may be unknown. But substituting \(\pi=\gamma/Z\) turns that unknown scale into an additive \(\log Z\). For a fixed target, this term is the same for every candidate, so it does not affect which variational parameters optimize the objective. Computing the numerical KL value and optimizing it are therefore different requirements.

Let us make that cancellation explicit, one step at a time.

From reverse KL to the ELBO

Keep the target fixed while we vary \(\phi\), with \(q_\phi\) a normalized density.

Start with the reverse KL we want to minimize:

\[ \mathrm{KL}(q_\phi\|\pi) =\mathbb E_{q_\phi}\!\left[\log q_\phi(z)-\log\pi(z)\right]. \]

Because \(\pi(z)=\gamma(z)/Z\), we have \(\log\pi(z)=\log\gamma(z)-\log Z\). Substituting, and keeping track of the minus sign, gives

\[ \mathrm{KL}(q_\phi\|\pi) =\mathbb E_{q_\phi}\!\left[\log q_\phi(z)-\log\gamma(z)+\log Z\right]. \]

The logarithm has turned the unknown multiplicative scale into an additive term. We have not computed \(Z\); we have isolated where it enters the objective.

For this fixed target, \(\log Z\) does not depend on \(z\). Since \(q_\phi\) integrates to one,

\[ \mathbb E_{q_\phi}[\log Z] =\log Z\int q_\phi(z)\,dz =\log Z. \]

So, even though the averaging distribution changes with \(\phi\), this particular expectation stays constant. We obtain

\[ \mathrm{KL}(q_\phi\|\pi) =\log Z+\mathbb E_{q_\phi}[\log q_\phi(z)-\log\gamma(z)]. \]

Reverse the signs inside the remaining expectation and define

\[ \boxed{\mathcal L(\phi) =\mathbb E_{q_\phi}[\log\gamma(z)-\log q_\phi(z)].} \]

There is also an intuitive way to read these two terms:

\[ \mathcal L(\phi)=\underbrace{\mathbb E_{q_\phi}[\log\gamma(z)]}_{\text{favor high target density}} +\underbrace{\mathcal H(q_\phi)}_{\text{retain spread}}, \qquad \mathcal H(q_\phi)=-\mathbb E_{q_\phi}[\log q_\phi(z)]. \]

The first term rewards putting probability in regions where the target density is high. The entropy term rewards retaining spread. Together, they balance concentrating in plausible regions with representing uncertainty, rather than simply selecting one high-density point. Spreading out indiscriminately is not rewarded overall: it can move probability into regions with low target density and worsen the first term.

A note on entropy for continuous distributions

For continuous distributions, \(\mathcal H\) is called differential entropy. For a Gaussian, \(\mathcal H(\mathcal N(m,s^2))=\tfrac12\log(2\pi e s^2)\), so increasing its spread increases its entropy. Shrinking its spread to zero drives that entropy to minus infinity.

This quantity is the evidence lower bound, or ELBO, when our target is a Bayesian posterior. The same objective also applies to a general unnormalized target. Substitution gives the central identity:

\[ \boxed{\log Z=\mathcal L(\phi)+\mathrm{KL}(q_\phi\|\pi).} \]

Notice what the ELBO requires: evaluations of \(\log\gamma\), evaluations of \(\log q_\phi\), and an expectation under \(q_\phi\). It does not require the value of \(Z\).

For a fixed target, \(\log Z\) is a fixed level. The ELBO sits below it, and the distance between them is exactly the reverse KL. If we raise the ELBO by one unit, the KL falls by one unit:

\[ \mathcal L(\phi_2)-\mathcal L(\phi_1) =\mathrm{KL}(q_{\phi_1}\|\pi)-\mathrm{KL}(q_{\phi_2}\|\pi). \]

Therefore the same parameters solve both optimization problems:

\[ \operatorname*{arg\,max}_{\phi}\mathcal L(\phi) =\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{\phi}\mathrm{KL}(q_\phi\|\pi). \]

We do not need to know the height of the fixed level to move toward it. Comparing ELBO values is enough to tell which candidate has the smaller reverse KL.

KL divergence is nonnegative, so the identity immediately implies

\[ \mathcal L(\phi)\leq\log Z. \]

In the Bayesian case, \(\gamma(z)=p_\theta(x,z)\) and \(Z=p_\theta(x)\). Consequently,

\[ \mathcal L(\phi) =\mathbb E_{q_\phi}[\log p_\theta(x,z)-\log q_\phi(z)] \leq\log p_\theta(x). \]

The ELBO is thus a lower bound on the log evidence. Equality holds when \(q_\phi\) matches the posterior. If the variational family cannot represent the posterior, even its best member leaves a gap. An imperfect optimization procedure can leave an additional gap.

We have now transformed the original inference problem into an optimization problem:

\[ \phi^\star\in\arg\max_\phi\mathcal L(\phi). \]

But this does not mean that the problem is solved. Depending on the target distribution and the variational family, evaluating the ELBO, calculating its derivatives, and finding its optimum may still be computationally difficult.

This is where variational inference algorithms begin to diverge. Some exploit conditional independence or conjugacy in the probabilistic model. Others exploit the structure of the variational family, identities for differentiating expectations, or techniques for reducing the variance of stochastic gradients.

The question guiding the rest of this article is therefore: What mathematical structure does each method exploit, what does it change, and which difficulty does that change resolve?

A connected reading of variational inference

Each entry makes two things explicit: the setup it assumes and the contribution it makes. A factorization of the model describes how the unknowns and observations depend on one another. A factorization of \(q_\phi\) describes the approximation we choose. These are different decisions.

From there, a paper may change the approximation family, the objective, the gradient estimator, or the optimization procedure. Others analyze an existing method’s error or convergence. Keeping these distinctions separate lets us see which ideas can be combined: a richer family can use a better gradient estimator without changing the target. 1987Mean-field approximation

A Mean Field Theory Learning Algorithm for Neural Networks

The move. Replace sampled Boltzmann-machine correlations with deterministic mean-field equations for learning.

Read with CAVI · Normalizing flows · Hierarchical VI

The idea and the mathematics

Learning a Boltzmann machine requires correlations between its binary units. Estimating those correlations by repeated stochastic simulation is expensive. Peterson and Anderson replace this sampling step with deterministic mean-field equations.

Replace fluctuating neighbors by their averages. In our notation, let the binary units be \(z_j\in\{-1,1\}\), with means \(m_j=\mathbb E_q[z_j]\). For symmetric couplings \(W_{jk}\), biases \(b_j\), and temperature \(T\gt 0\), the mean-field equations take the form

\[ m_j=\tanh\!\left(\frac{b_j+\sum_{k\ne j}W_{jk}m_k}{T}\right), \qquad \mathbb E_q[z_jz_k]=m_jm_k\quad(j\ne k). \]

Each unit responds to the average field produced by the others. The resulting products of means approximate the correlations used in the learning rule, in both the clamped and free phases. This is the paper's computational change: deterministic approximate correlations replace expensive stochastic measurements.

The transferable variational idea is to fit independent factors:

\[ q_\phi(z)=\prod_{j=1}^Jq_{\phi_j}(z_j). \]

We abbreviate each factor as \(q_j\). Under \(q_1(z_1)q_2(z_2)\), drawing a large \(z_1\) does not make a large \(z_2\) more likely. Each block can still contain several dependent unknowns; independence is imposed between blocks.

This makes sampling and density evaluation simple: draw each block separately and add their log densities. The target still couples the optimization problems, so the best choice for one factor depends on the others. MFVI chooses the family; CAVI or gradient methods choose its parameters.

The cost is lost geometry. A correlated Gaussian has tilted contours, whereas a product of scalar Gaussians has axis-aligned contours.

For example,

\[ \pi=\mathcal N\!\left(0,\begin{bmatrix}1&\rho\\\rho&1\end{bmatrix}\right), \qquad q^\star=\mathcal N\!\left(0,(1-\rho^2)I\right). \]

The true marginal variances are \(1\), but at \(\rho=0.8\) the fitted variances are \(0.36\). Widening independent factors would put mass outside the narrow tilted region, so reverse KL favors a narrower approximation.

When it helps. Mean field offers inexpensive inference when dependencies are weak or speed matters. Strong correlations can make uncertainty too narrow, and separated modes can lead to a fit around just one mode. Larger blocks, flows, or hierarchical approximations can retain more structure.

What carries forward. The paper applies mean field to Boltzmann-machine learning. The Gaussian illustration above isolates the same independence tradeoff in a different model. More generally, choosing larger independent blocks preserves dependence inside them. The model's variables need not be independent just because the approximation is.

The idea and the mathematics

Sigmoid belief networks are directed models of binary variables. Each unit's probability depends nonlinearly on its parents, so computing the likelihood of observed units can require summing over exponentially many hidden configurations. Saul, Jaakkola, and Jordan develop a tractable mean-field lower bound for these networks.

For hidden units \(z_j\in\{0,1\}\), use Bernoulli factors

Write \(q_{-j}=\prod_{k\ne j}q_k\). The core update is

\[ \boxed{ q_j^{(t+1)}(z_j)\propto \exp\!\left(\mathbb E_{q_{-j}^{(t)}}[\log\gamma(z)]\right). } \]

Variational Message Passing

The move. Express CAVI as reusable local messages between neighboring parts of a conjugate-exponential model.

Read with CAVI · Nonconjugate VI · Expectation propagation · Graphical VI foundations · Quadratic logistic bounds · Nonconjugate messages

The idea and the mathematics

CAVI gives us a factor update, but deriving a separate implementation for every model is tedious. Variational message passing turns that update into a reusable computation on a graph.

Suppose the model has local factors \(\gamma(z)=\prod_a\psi_a(z_a)\), where \(z_a\) contains the variables used by factor \(a\). Keep the mean-field approximation \(q_\phi(z)=\prod_jq_j(z_j)\). Only factors touching \(z_j\) can affect its update:

\[ \log q_j^\star(z_j) =\sum_{a:j\in a}\underbrace{\mathbb E_{q_{-j}}[\log\psi_a(z_a)]}_{\text{message from factor }a} +c_j. \]

Here \(c_j\) makes the density integrate to one. The expectation averages the other variables in each neighboring factor. A distant factor contributes nothing that varies with \(z_j\).

The graph tells us who needs to communicate. In a chain with factors \(\psi_{12}(z_1,z_2)\) and \(\psi_{23}(z_2,z_3)\), updating \(q_2\) combines two messages. Updating \(q_1\) only needs the first. Information still travels farther over successive updates, because the neighboring distributions themselves change.

Conjugate-exponential structure makes these messages compact. If the terms depending on \(z_j\) are linear in sufficient statistics \(T_j(z_j)\), their coefficient vectors add:

\[ q_j^\star(z_j)\propto h_j(z_j) \exp\!\left\{T_j(z_j)^\top \sum_{a:j\in a}\mathbb E_{q_{-j}}[\lambda_{a\to j}]\right\}. \]

The \(\lambda_{a\to j}\) are the natural-parameter contributions of neighboring factors; \(h_j\) is the base measure. Software can implement these local rules once and reuse them across models.

What changes. The ELBO and mean-field family stay in place. Model factorization localizes the computation; conjugacy turns it into moment and natural-parameter messages. Exact sequential factor updates retain CAVI's ascent property.

This is useful for assembling large models from familiar probabilistic components. It does not restore dependence removed by mean field. Nonconjugate factors need additional approximations or bounds; see nonconjugate VI. Expectation propagation also passes local information, but uses a different projection.

2013Approximate coordinate updates

Variational Inference in Nonconjugate Models

The move. Use local Taylor approximations when a nonconjugate factor prevents an analytic coordinate update.

Read with CAVI · VMP · Stochastic search VI · Doubly stochastic VI · Sparse GP VI · Graphical VI foundations · Quadratic logistic bounds · Nonconjugate messages · GP classification

The idea and the mathematics

The CAVI formula can be correct yet unusable: exponentiating an expected log joint need not produce a density we know how to normalize. Wang and Blei address this obstacle with local quadratic approximations for continuous nonconjugate variables.

For one such block \(z_j\), define

\[ f_j(z_j)=\mathbb E_{q_{-j}}[\log\gamma(z)]. \]

The ideal update is proportional to \(\exp f_j\). Laplace variational inference finds a local maximizer \(m_j\) of \(f_j\) and uses its curvature:

\[ q_j(z_j)\approx\mathcal N(z_j\mid m_j,\Sigma_j), \qquad \Sigma_j=[-\nabla^2f_j(m_j)]^{-1}. \]

A quadratic log density is Gaussian. The Hessian tells us how quickly the expected log joint falls away from its maximum: steep directions get smaller variances. Other factors are updated using expectations under this Gaussian, and the process repeats.

Delta-method VI approximates the expectation instead. For a Gaussian block,

\[ \mathbb E_{q_j}[f_j(z_j)] \approx f_j(m_j)+\tfrac12\operatorname{tr} \!\left(\nabla^2f_j(m_j)\Sigma_j\right). \]

Adding the Gaussian entropy gives a tractable approximate objective to optimize over \(m_j,\Sigma_j\). The distinction is where the Taylor expansion enters: a coordinate density in Laplace VI, or an expected objective in delta-method VI.

For intuition, consider \(f_j(z_j)=-a(z_j-b)^2/2+c\), with \(a\gt 0\). Both approaches recover a Gaussian centered at \(b\) with variance \(1/a\). If the actual log density bends differently away from \(b\), the same quadratic becomes a local description whose quality depends on how much probability lies beyond that neighborhood.

What changes. Mean-field factorization remains; difficult coordinate calculations are approximated. The paper develops this within models combining smooth nonconjugate blocks and tractable exponential-family structure. It is useful for problems such as Bayesian logistic regression, but a local Gaussian can miss skewness, heavy tails, or separated modes. Approximate updates need not increase the original ELBO.

Stochastic search offers a contrasting response: estimate the missing calculation and correct its bias rather than optimize a Taylor approximation.

2013Optimization

Stochastic Variational Inference

The move. Replace the full-data coordinate update with a minibatch estimate of its natural-gradient direction.

Read with CAVI · BBVI · Joint control variate · Structured SVI · Sparse GP VI · Doubly stochastic VI · Collapsed optimization · Variational tempering · Mixture natural gradients · Minibatch sparse GPs

The idea and the mathematics

CAVI solves one factor at a time, but a global factor may depend on information from millions of observations. Recomputing every local factor before each global update defeats the purpose of having simple coordinates. SVI keeps the variational objective and asks whether a small, random set of local contributions can give a useful global update.

\[ \gamma(z)=p_\theta(x,z) =p_\theta(z_g)\prod_{i=1}^N p_\theta(x_i,z_i\mid z_g). \]

Given the global unknown, the observation–local-variable pairs separate. In a topic model, the topics are global, while each document has its own topic proportions and assignments. This is an assumption about how the data are generated.

We approximate the posterior using a global factor and one factor for each observation:

\[ q_\phi(z)=q_{\phi_g}(z_g)\prod_{i=1}^Nq_{\phi_i}(z_i). \]

This is a separate assumption: the approximation makes the global block and the local blocks independent, even though the posterior generally couples them.

The factorization tells us which distributions to fit, but not whether their updates are computable. The original SVI construction therefore focuses on models with conditionally conjugate exponential-family distributions. We can unpack this requirement in two steps.

An exponential family expresses a density through a fixed collection of functions of its variable, with adjustable coefficients. We choose the global variational factor in the form

\[ q_{\phi_g}(z_g)=h(z_g)\exp\{\phi_g^\top T_g(z_g)-A(\phi_g)\}. \]

Here \(T_g\) collects sufficient statistics, \(\phi_g\) is their coefficient vector (the natural parameter), \(h\) is a fixed base measure, and \(A\) is the log normalizer that makes the density integrate to one. For a Gaussian, the statistics are \(z_g\) and \(z_g^2\), and the natural parameters are \(m_g/v_g\) and \(-1/(2v_g)\). So averaging natural parameters is generally different from averaging means and variances.

Conditional conjugacy means that conditioning on the other unknowns and the observations leaves the global variable in the matching family. This conditional distribution is called its complete conditional. In the model class considered here, it has the form

\[ \log p_\theta(z_g\mid x,z_{1:N}) =\log h(z_g)+ \left[\alpha_0+\sum_i T_i(x_i,z_i)\right]^\top T_g(z_g) +\text{constant in }z_g. \]

Here \(\alpha_0\) is the prior natural parameter and \(T_i(x_i,z_i)\) is observation \(i\)'s contribution to it. In the CAVI update, taking the expectation over the local factors only changes the coefficients: \(T_i\) becomes \(\bar T_i=\mathbb E_{q_i}[T_i(x_i,z_i)]\). The functional form survives. That is why a whole distribution update reduces to adding a few expected statistics. The vector \(T_i\) includes any count terms needed by the conjugate family. Matching exponential-family local conditionals likewise makes local coordinate updates tractable; a local block may still require several such updates.

CAVI can now require a pass through every observation before updating the global factor. In natural-parameter coordinates, its global optimum for fixed local factors takes the form

\[ \phi_g^\star=\alpha_0+\sum_{i=1}^N \bar T_i, \]

The full-data update is thus the prior contribution plus all the fitted local contributions.

Each local contribution depends on the current global belief. We therefore hold the global factor fixed and fit the local factors before collecting their expected statistics. Conceptually, this gives an objective depending only on the global parameters:

\[ \mathcal L_{\mathrm{prof}}(\phi_g) =\max_{\phi_1,\ldots,\phi_N} \mathcal L(\phi_g,\phi_1,\ldots,\phi_N). \]

In the document example, each local fit uses the current global topic distribution before contributing its expected word counts. At an exact local optimum, the local first-order derivatives vanish, so the global derivative can be evaluated holding those optimized local parameters fixed. We do not need to differentiate through every local optimization step.

To turn these best responses into an optimization direction, SVI uses a natural gradient: a gradient adjusted for how parameter changes alter the distribution. The Fisher information matrix \(I(\phi_g)\) measures this local geometry. For our exponential-family factor,

\[ I(\phi_g)=\nabla^2 A(\phi_g) =\operatorname{Cov}_{q_{\phi_g}}[T_g(z_g)], \qquad \nabla_{\phi_g}\mathcal L_{\mathrm{prof}} =I(\phi_g)\left(\alpha_0+\sum_i\bar T_i-\phi_g\right). \]

This is the second place the exponential-family structure pays off: the ordinary gradient contains exactly the Fisher matrix. The natural gradient cancels that matrix, leaving

\[ I(\phi_g)^{-1}\nabla_{\phi_g}\mathcal L_{\mathrm{prof}} =\alpha_0+\sum_i \bar T_i-\phi_g. \]

A minibatch estimates the whole sum. For a uniformly sampled batch \(\mathcal B\), rescale its contribution:

\[ \widehat\phi_g^{(t)}=\alpha_0+\frac{N}{|\mathcal B|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal B}\bar T_i^{(t)}, \qquad \phi_g^{(t+1)}=(1-\eta_t)\phi_g^{(t)}+\eta_t\widehat\phi_g^{(t)}. \]

At iteration \(t\), \(\bar T_i^{(t)}\) uses the current local fit, and \(\widehat\phi_g^{(t)}\) is the resulting minibatch proposal.

The factor \(N/|\mathcal B|\) puts a small batch on the scale of the full dataset. The step size \(\eta_t\) prevents one noisy batch from replacing everything learned so far.

The minibatch update estimates that direction. Compared with CAVI on this same model and family, the objective and model structure remain; the expensive full-data update becomes stochastic. BBVI introduces a different source of randomness: draws of the unknowns from \(q_\phi\). SVI's conjugate expectations can be computed analytically, so stochastic optimization here need not involve sampling the latent variables at all. Joint control variates address both sources together.

Think of each local fit as a message to the global fit. In a topic model, a document's local factors describe which topics explain its words. Their expected word counts become evidence for the shared topics. A minibatch sends only a few such messages, scaled up to estimate the messages from the whole corpus. Repeating this over different documents builds a global picture without rereading the entire corpus at every step.

The natural gradient makes the connection to CAVI particularly clean: it points from the current natural parameter toward the coordinate optimum. A full step with all observations recovers that coordinate update; a smaller stochastic step moves toward an estimated optimum. The geometry matters because equal changes in a distribution's raw parameters need not represent equal changes in the distribution.

Exponential-family notation alone is not enough. A Gaussian approximation to a nonconjugate model does not automatically give the additive update above. Conjugacy supplies the matching log-density form; the exponential family supplies the coefficient representation and Fisher identity; the global–local decomposition supplies the sum we can subsample.

When this helps. The data must have exploitable local/global structure, and the conjugate updates must be available. The expensive part becomes fitting the local factors for the selected observations. Inaccurate local fits can spoil the gradient estimate; a nonconjugate model does not automatically inherit these closed-form directions. Unlike exact CAVI, an individual noisy minibatch update can lower the ELBO. Progress emerges across updates with a suitable step-size schedule.

The broad idea of stochastic optimization applies much more widely. But using SGD on an ELBO is not enough to inherit this paper's local fitting rule, sufficient-statistic sum, or natural-gradient formula. Those come from the particular model and variational structure above.

What carries forward. Three ingredients should be separated: conditional independence creates an additive data sum; conjugacy makes the required expected sufficient statistics tractable; exponential-family geometry turns the CAVI difference into a natural gradient. Minibatching is reusable beyond these assumptions, but the particular SVI formula is not.

Stochastic Structured Variational Inference

The move. Let each local approximation respond to a sampled global state while retaining stochastic global updates.

Read with SVI · Hierarchical VI · Sparse GP VI · Geometry of VI · Collapsed LDA · Variational tempering

The idea and the mathematics

In SVI, a local factor must work across all plausible values of the global variable without depending on which value actually occurred. That can be restrictive. In a mixture model, for example, an observation's cluster assignment should respond to the sampled cluster locations.

Structured SVI retains the global–local model but changes the approximation:

\[ q_{\phi_g}(z_g)\prod_{i=1}^Nq_{\phi_i}(z_i) \quad\longrightarrow\quad q_{\phi_g}(z_g)\prod_{i=1}^Nq_\phi(z_i\mid z_g). \]

The right-hand side can represent global–local dependence. Local blocks remain conditionally independent given \(z_g\); the paper also retains a factorization between global blocks inside \(q_{\phi_g}\).

Fit each local problem in the global state that was drawn. Sample \(z_g\) from its current approximation. For each selected observation, fit or sample a local conditional approximation at that value of \(z_g\). Its sufficient statistics then inform the global update. Different global draws can produce different local explanations of the same observation.

The decomposition makes the benefit precise:

\[ \mathrm{KL}(q_\phi(z)\|\pi(z)) =\mathrm{KL}(q_{\phi_g}(z_g)\|\pi(z_g)) +\sum_i\mathbb E_{q_{\phi_g}} \mathrm{KL}\!\left(q_\phi(z_i\mid z_g)\| p(z_i\mid x_i,z_g)\right). \]

If the local conditionals are exact, the second term vanishes. Global fitting then targets the marginal posterior over \(z_g\), rather than a joint approximation constrained by global–local independence.

The update changes too. The paper retains a conjugate-exponential global conditional. With global natural parameters \(\phi_g\), prior parameters \(\lambda_0\), and local statistic estimates \(\widehat s_i\), its minibatch natural-gradient step has the form

\[ \phi_g^{(t+1)}=(1-\eta_t)\phi_g^{(t)} +\eta_t\!\left[\lambda_0+ V(z_g,\phi_g^{(t)})\frac{N}{|\mathcal B|} \sum_{i\in\mathcal B}\widehat s_i\right]. \]

The matrix \(V\) accounts for how the sampled global state moves with its variational parameters. The simpler SSVI-A drops this correction, introducing an approximation.

This is valuable when local decisions depend strongly on global uncertainty. It still requires tractable or sufficiently accurate local inference and a suitable global family. Expensive conditional fits or poorly mixed local MCMC can erase the computational benefit. The change is to the variational dependence and its optimization, not the model's latent-variable structure.

2012Unbiased gradient estimation

Variational Bayesian Inference with Stochastic Search

The move. Use a tractable approximation as a control variate and estimate its residual, retaining the original ELBO.

Read with BBVI · Nonconjugate VI · Overdispersed BBVI · Learned quadratic controls

The idea and the mathematics

When an ELBO contains an intractable expectation, one response is to replace it with a tractable bound. Paisley and colleagues instead use that approximation to make an unbiased stochastic gradient less noisy.

Suppose the difficult term is \(\mathbb E_{q_\phi}[f(z)]\). Find a correlated surrogate \(h(z)\) whose expectation and derivative are tractable. With its parameters and coefficient \(a\) fixed during the gradient calculation,

\[ \nabla_\phi\mathbb E_{q_\phi}[f] =a\nabla_\phi\mathbb E_{q_\phi}[h] +\mathbb E_{q_\phi}[(f-ah)S_\phi], \qquad S_\phi=\nabla_\phi\log q_\phi. \]

The first term is computed analytically. Monte Carlo estimates only the residual term.

Use the approximation as a prediction, then correct its error. If \(h\) tracks the variation in \(f\), then \(f-ah\) fluctuates less than \(f\). The analytically integrated part carries most of the gradient, while samples supply what it missed.

For example, write a smooth scalar function around \(b\) as

\[ f(z)=\underbrace{f(b)+f'(b)(z-b)+ \tfrac12f''(b)(z-b)^2}_{h(z)} +r(z). \]

Under a Gaussian approximation, the quadratic expectation is analytic. Sampling \(r(z)S_\phi(z)\) corrects the Taylor approximation. If \(f\) is exactly quadratic and \(a=1\), the residual vanishes.

What changes. The original ELBO is retained. Unlike delta-method VI, the surrogate does not become the final optimization objective. It need not even be a lower bound; correlation and tractable expectations are what matter.

This is especially useful for nonconjugate terms embedded in an otherwise tractable model. The paper develops stochastic search with control variates and adapts sample effort to gradient noise. It is a direct conceptual companion to BBVI and later approximation-based controls.

A poor surrogate can leave substantial residual noise, and fitting the control adds overhead. Coefficients estimated from the same draws used for the final gradient can introduce bias, so pilot estimates or an appropriate correction are needed. Preserving the expected gradient removes approximation bias from this calculation; it does not remove restrictions of the variational family.

Black Box Variational Inference

The move. Replace model-specific coordinate algebra with a score-function estimate of the same ELBO gradient.

Read with CAVI · AEVB · NVIL · Overdispersed BBVI · Stochastic search VI · Quadratic logistic bounds · Black-box boosting

The idea and the mathematics

SVI scales an update that the model's conjugacy already makes available. What if the model does not yield an analytic coordinate update at all? Rather than solving for each new factor, BBVI uses a general identity for differentiating an expectation with respect to the distribution doing the averaging. That identity creates a gradient estimator from samples of our own approximation.

What if the coordinate expectation cannot be integrated analytically? We can still draw from \(q_\phi\) and estimate a gradient. Define its score

\[ S_\phi(z)=\nabla_\phi\log q_\phi(z). \]

This tells us how the log density at a fixed point changes when we adjust the approximation. Using \(\nabla_\phi q_\phi=q_\phi S_\phi\), differentiate the ELBO:

\[ \begin{aligned} \nabla_\phi\mathcal L &=\int q_\phi(z)S_\phi(z) [\log\gamma(z)-\log q_\phi(z)]\,dz -\int q_\phi(z)S_\phi(z)\,dz\\ &=\mathbb E_{q_\phi}[\log w_\phi(z)\,S_\phi(z)]. \end{aligned} \]

The last integral vanishes because \(\mathbb E_{q_\phi}[S_\phi]=\nabla_\phi\int q_\phi(z)\,dz=0\). So samples from the approximation give the estimate

\[ \widehat g_\phi=\frac1K\sum_{s=1}^K \log w_\phi(z^{(s)})S_\phi(z^{(s)}), \qquad z^{(s)}\sim q_\phi. \]

The log weight measures how the target and approximation compare; the score tells us how to change the approximation's density at the sampled point. Their product turns the comparison into an update direction.

The estimate can be noisy. The paper reduces this noise with control variates and by averaging out irrelevant variables (Rao–Blackwellization). The new ingredient is an estimated gradient of the same ELBO. AEVB obtains that gradient by moving the samples instead of differentiating their density.

An example of the noise we can remove. Suppose one coordinate of \(z\) explains only a small part of the model, but its score is multiplied by the log joint for everything. Random changes in unrelated terms then perturb that coordinate's update without providing useful information about it. Rao–Blackwellization removes terms whose expected contribution is zero, leaving the relevant local terms. It is a way of asking each factor to respond to the evidence that actually concerns it.

Similarly, for a constant \(b\),

\[ \mathbb E_q[(\log w_\phi-b)S_\phi] =\mathbb E_q[\log w_\phi S_\phi]. \]

Subtracting a baseline changes the random fluctuations but not their average. A good baseline makes the learning signal describe whether a sample is better or worse than expected, rather than carrying a large common offset.

What black box buys us—and what it costs. We need samples, log-density evaluations, and the variational score, without a hand-derived coordinate update or a derivative of the model with respect to \(z\). This makes discrete variables possible too. But a very noisy learning signal, particularly in many dimensions, can make optimization slow. The estimator also learns only from regions visited by \(q_\phi\); unbiased gradients do not guarantee that a poorly initialized approximation discovers a distant mode.

What carries forward. The reusable identity is the score-function trick: it applies well beyond Bayesian posteriors and can handle discrete variables. The black-box promise has a boundary: we still need to evaluate the log target, sample and score the variational family, and manage potentially severe gradient noise.

Auto-Encoding Variational Bayes

The move. Combine pathwise ELBO gradients with an inference network for joint inference and generative-model learning.

Read with BBVI · Stochastic backpropagation · Sticking the Landing · Amortization gap · Adversarial VI · Doubly stochastic VI · Bayes by Backprop

The idea and the mathematics

BBVI obtains gradients by asking how a density changes at a fixed sampled point. For smooth continuous variables, we can ask a different question: if the same underlying random noise were reused, how would the sampled value move when the parameters change? AEVB uses that motion to differentiate the ELBO, and learns an inference rule shared across observations.

So far, we have fitted an approximate posterior for a given model. AEVB also learns the model itself: \(\theta\) controls the generative model, while \(\phi\) controls the inference network. For one observation, the same ELBO now has two sets of parameters:

\[ \mathcal L(\theta,\phi;x) =\mathbb E_{q_\phi(z\mid x)} \left[\log p_\theta(x,z)-\log q_\phi(z\mid x)\right]. \]

Training maximizes the sum of these objectives over the observations, adjusting both \(\theta\) and \(\phi\). Changing \(\phi\) improves the approximation to the current model's posterior; changing \(\theta\) changes the generative model and therefore the posterior being approximated. In a VAE, these are the encoder and decoder parameters, respectively.

The identity from the foundation still holds:

\[ \log p_\theta(x)=\mathcal L(\theta,\phi;x) +\mathrm{KL}\!\left(q_\phi(z\mid x)\,\|\,p_\theta(z\mid x)\right). \]

For a fixed \(\theta\), increasing the ELBO decreases this KL by the same amount. During model learning, the evidence \(\log p_\theta(x)\) also changes, so the target is no longer a fixed level.

Could we infer the model parameters too?

Yes. We can place a prior on the model parameters and approximate their posterior jointly with the latent variables. For a dataset \(x_{1:N}\), this means fitting an approximation to \(p(\theta,z_{1:N}\mid x_{1:N})\). Collecting all these unknowns into \(u=(\theta,z_{1:N})\) brings us directly back to the generic inference problem in the foundation.

This fully Bayesian alternative represents uncertainty about the model weights. A standard VAE instead learns a single set of weights \(\theta\), while its inference network approximates uncertainty about the latent variables.

In BBVI, we differentiate the distribution that produces a draw. Here we express the draw itself as a differentiable function:

\[ \epsilon\sim\nu,\qquad z=T_\phi(x,\epsilon). \]

When the observation is explicit, write \(q_\phi(z\mid x)\); for fixed \(x\), this is the same \(q_\phi(z)\) used throughout.

The noise law \(\nu\) stays fixed. For a diagonal Gaussian approximation,

\[ z=m_\phi(x)+s_\phi(x)\odot\epsilon, \qquad \epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I). \]

Hold the same noise draw while changing \(\phi\). The resulting sample moves smoothly, so ordinary differentiation can follow its movement:

\[ \mathcal L(\theta,\phi;x)= \mathbb E_{\epsilon\sim\nu}\!\left[ \log p_\theta(x,T_\phi(x,\epsilon)) -\log q_\phi(T_\phi(x,\epsilon)\mid x)\right], \]
\[ \nabla_\phi\mathcal L(\theta,\phi;x)= \mathbb E_{\epsilon\sim\nu}\!\left[ \frac{d}{d\phi}\left\{ \log p_\theta(x,T_\phi(x,\epsilon)) -\log q_\phi(T_\phi(x,\epsilon)\mid x)\right\} \right]. \]

This is a pathwise gradient. It uses the slope of the target along the moving sample, rather than only a density comparison at a fixed point.

The inference network predicts \(m_\phi(x)\) and \(s_\phi(x)\) for each observation. This is amortization—learning how to infer across many observations. The derivative above holds \(\theta\) fixed while differentiating with respect to \(\phi\); training also computes a derivative with respect to \(\theta\) to update the generative model.

Stochastic backpropagation develops a closely related route. Sticking the Landing removes a noisy term from this derivative. Inference suboptimality asks what is lost when an encoder chooses the local approximation.

Follow one draw. For a scalar \(z=m+s\epsilon\) and a fixed smooth function \(f\),

\[ \partial_m\mathbb E[f(z)]=\mathbb E[f'(m+s\epsilon)], \qquad \partial_s\mathbb E[f(z)]=\mathbb E[f'(m+s\epsilon)\epsilon]. \]

Increasing \(m\) moves every draw right. Increasing \(s\) moves positive-noise draws right and negative-noise draws left. The derivatives ask whether these movements improve the function. For \(f(z)=z^2\), the sampled mean-gradient is \(2(m+s\epsilon)\), whose average is \(2m\). Holding the noise fixed gives a meaningful comparison between nearby parameter settings.

In a VAE, the same mechanism tells the encoder how changing a latent code affects reconstruction. The ELBO balances expected log likelihood against the KL to the prior: useful codes should explain the observation while keeping the approximate posterior compatible with the generative model.

Where the difficulties move. A hard discrete draw does not move smoothly, motivating Concrete and discrete estimators. Even with smooth draws, steep decoder derivatives can produce noisy gradients. And a fast encoder can return a suboptimal local distribution: reparameterization makes its gradient tractable, while amortization-gap methods address how well its output actually solves inference.

What carries forward. Two ideas can be reused independently: pathwise differentiation is a gradient-estimation method, while amortization replaces separately fitted local parameters by a shared function of the observation. A model can use one without the other.

The idea and the mathematics

AEVB gave us a concrete way to differentiate through Gaussian noise. Stochastic backpropagation develops the same mathematical direction from Gaussian expectation identities and extends it across deep networks of random variables. The challenge is not just one noisy layer: an upstream change affects the distribution of every downstream layer.

An independent route to similar gradient machinery starts with Gaussian differentiation identities. Here \(m\) and \(\Sigma\) are the mean and covariance components of \(\phi\). For smooth \(f\) and \(z\sim\mathcal N(m,\Sigma)\),

\[ \nabla_m\mathbb E[f(z)]=\mathbb E[\nabla_z f(z)], \qquad d_\Sigma\mathbb E[f(z)] =\tfrac12\operatorname{tr}\!\left(\mathbb E[\nabla_z^2 f(z)]\,d\Sigma\right). \]

The first formula moves a derivative with respect to the Gaussian mean onto the function being averaged. A location-scale representation also gives first-order derivatives through a factor of \(\Sigma\). The paper develops these ideas for backpropagation through layers of latent Gaussian variables. AEVB is the simplest entry point for the sampling transformation. This paper makes the Gaussian calculus behind it explicit.

Why the two Gaussian identities make sense. Moving the mean translates the whole cloud, so the average slope of \(f\) determines the change. Changing the covariance spreads the cloud instead. Whether spreading helps depends on curvature: a convex bowl rewards spread in its upward-curving directions, whereas a concave hill penalizes it. For the scalar example \(f(z)=z^2\), we have \(\mathbb E[f]=m^2+v\). The mean derivative is \(2m\), and the variance derivative is \(1=\tfrac12 f''\).

Computing a full Hessian would be expensive in a large latent space. Instead, write \(\Sigma=BB^\top\) and \(z=m+B\epsilon\). Then

\[ \nabla_B\mathbb E[f(z)] =\mathbb E[\nabla_z f(m+B\epsilon)\epsilon^\top]. \]

This replaces the covariance derivative involving second derivatives with a scale-factor derivative involving first derivatives. Repeating the chain rule through successive latent Gaussian layers makes deep stochastic models trainable with backpropagation.

When it helps. The method is especially natural when Gaussian noise enters smooth neural computations. The Gaussian identities do not directly solve discrete sampling, and a simple Gaussian recognition model may still miss posterior dependencies or multiple modes. This paper supplies gradient machinery; flows later enrich the distributions that machinery can train.

What carries forward. Its Gaussian differentiation identities remain useful whenever smooth functions are averaged against Gaussian laws, including models without a VAE encoder. They are identities for derivatives of expectations, not a new divergence or a universal solution to discrete randomness.

The idea and the mathematics

The move. Combine score-function and pathwise contributions in a single computation graph.

The idea and the mathematics

We now have two gradient routes: change the density, or move the sample. A real probabilistic program may contain both discrete draws and differentiable continuous operations. Stochastic computation graphs explain how the derivatives of those pieces fit together, and which future costs each random choice is responsible for.

Factor a computation into conditional draws \(v\sim p(v\mid\mathrm{parents})\) and deterministic operations. Differentiating the joint sampling density gives a score contribution for each stochastic node. Only costs downstream of that node need to multiply its score, since the expected product with an unaffected cost vanishes after conditioning on the appropriate predecessors. Differentiable paths contribute ordinary chain-rule terms. BBVI and AEVB can therefore appear in different parts of the same model. This is especially helpful when the model has both continuous and discrete random variables.

Consider a discrete gate followed by a continuous computation. Draw \(z\sim q_\phi\), compute \(h_\phi(z)\), and evaluate a cost \(c(h_\phi(z))\). There are two ways \(\phi\) changes the average cost: it changes how often each gate is chosen, and it changes the computation after a gate has been chosen. Thus

\[ \nabla_\phi\mathbb E_q[c(h_\phi(z))] =\mathbb E_q\!\left[ c(h_\phi(z))S_\phi(z) +\left.\nabla_\phi c(h_\phi(z))\right|_{z\;\mathrm{fixed}} \right]. \]

The first term handles the random choice; the second is ordinary backpropagation. Ignoring the first would never teach the model which choices to make. Including unrelated costs in it would add noise without useful credit assignment.

The paper organizes these rules into a graph-based surrogate whose derivatives produce the desired estimator. This makes mixed stochastic programs easier to implement and explains where baselines can depend on earlier information.

Its scope. This is a framework for constructing correct gradients, not a promise that they have low variance. A long downstream sequence can give a very noisy score signal; a long differentiable path can have exploding derivatives. NVIL, RELAX, and the pathwise methods can be understood as improving particular pieces of this graph.

What carries forward. This is a general calculus for stochastic programs, not a new VI objective. It lets an implementation mix score and pathwise terms and use the graph's dependency structure to remove irrelevant learning signals.

Monte Carlo Gradient Estimation in Machine Learning

The idea and the mathematics

At this point, the research papers may look like unrelated recipes for gradients. The survey gives us a better map: a derivative of an expectation can be represented by changes in density, by motion of samples, or by differences between probability measures. The quantity being differentiated is the same; the estimator and its noise differ.

The survey distinguishes three representations of the same derivative. The score representation differentiates the density. The pathwise representation moves samples and differentiates the integrand. A measure-valued representation writes a derivative of a probability measure as \(c(\mu^+-\mu^-)\), giving \(c(\mathbb E_{\mu^+}f-\mathbb E_{\mu^-}f)\). The third perspective is especially useful when you can evaluate a function at coupled draws even when the function itself is hard to differentiate. This provides the common vocabulary for comparing BBVI, AEVB, and the discrete estimators below.

A Bernoulli variable connects all three viewpoints. Write \(p=\operatorname{sigmoid}(\phi)\). For a fixed function \(f\),

\[ \partial_\phi\mathbb E[f(z)] =p(1-p)[f(1)-f(0)]. \]

The score estimator recovers this by randomly choosing an outcome and weighting its function value. A measure-valued derivative represents it directly as the difference between point masses at 1 and 0, scaled by \(p(1-p)\). A naive pathwise derivative through a threshold gives zero almost everywhere and misses the movement of probability across the threshold.

The survey's practical lesson is to choose a representation based on the computation available. Can we differentiate \(f\)? Can we evaluate it at paired samples? Can a baseline remove a large common offset? Is one estimator cheap enough to afford more draws? Its comparisons show why low variance alone is not the whole decision: derivative evaluations, additional function calls, and the behavior of the particular integrand also matter.

Smoothness favors pathwise methods, discrete or inaccessible derivatives favor score or measure-valued approaches, and effective coupling or control variates can change the comparison substantially.

What carries forward. Use this paper as a reference map rather than as another algorithm in the chronology. Its framework helps identify which assumptions—density scores, differentiable paths, or coupled function evaluations—make an estimator available.

1999Variational bounds and structure

An Introduction to Variational Methods for Graphical Models

The move. Explain how tractable bounds and simpler graphical structures turn difficult inference into variational optimization.

Read with CAVI · VMP · Nonconjugate VI · Geometry of VI

The idea and the mathematics

An inference calculation may be hard because a single local term couples many variables. This foundational treatment shows how variational bounds can replace such terms with tractable expressions and thereby simplify the graph.

Trade a difficult function for an adjustable bound. A concave function \(f\) lies below its tangent at \(\xi\):

\[ f(u)\leq f(\xi)+f'(\xi)(u-\xi). \]

If \(u=\sum_j a_jz_j\), the right-hand side is additive in the variables. Exponentiating it produces a product of separate terms. The new parameter \(\xi\) lets us choose where the approximation is tight.

For example, the log sigmoid \(f(u)=\log\sigma(u)\) is concave. Its tangent gives an upper bound on the sigmoid after exponentiation. This is useful for understanding how a nonlinear coupling can become separable. The direction of the bound matters: that particular construction is an upper bound, not automatically an ELBO.

The broader idea is convex duality: represent a function as the best member of a family of simpler functions. A chosen member is tractable; optimizing its variational parameter tightens the approximation.

Simplifying the approximation's graph is another choice. A structured family might retain a chain,

\[ q_\phi(z)\propto \prod_j r_j(z_j)\prod_j r_{j,j+1}(z_j,z_{j+1}), \]

while omitting harder long-range interactions. Exact inference inside this simpler chain can remain efficient even though the original model is difficult.

Imagine neighboring latent states in a time series. Fully independent factors cannot represent a tendency for consecutive states to agree. Keeping the chain preserves that behavior while sacrificing some other dependencies. The price is more work per variational update.

What this reading adds. This is a tutorial and synthesis, not a single new optimizer. It connects local functional bounds, tractable graphical structures, and variational optimization. These are different ways to make an inference computation manageable.

Loose bounds can distort the fit, and a simplified graph can omit important dependence. VMP turns suitable local calculations into reusable messages; nonconjugate VI uses local approximations when exact expectations fail. Stochastic search shows how an approximation can instead serve as a variance-reduction tool without replacing the intended objective.

2008Unifying mathematical framework

Graphical Models, Exponential Families, and Variational Inference

The move. Express inference through log-partition and entropy duality, separating restrictions of the feasible family from relaxations of the inference problem.

Read with CAVI · Structured SVI · Graphical VI foundations · Expectation propagation

The idea and the mathematics

Many inference methods appear unrelated because they optimize different objects: distributions, moments, messages, or local bounds. Wainwright and Jordan explain their common geometry through exponential families and entropy.

For clarity, take a finite discrete state space and write

\[ \pi_\lambda(z)=\exp\{\lambda^\top T(z)-A(\lambda)\}, \qquad A(\lambda)=\log\sum_z\exp\{\lambda^\top T(z)\}. \]

Here \(\lambda\) is the fixed target's natural parameter, \(T(z)\) its sufficient statistics, and \(A(\lambda)=\log Z\). The variational parameters remain \(\phi\); they are not \(\lambda\).

The log-partition function has the exact representation

\[ A(\lambda)=\sup_q \{\lambda^\top\mathbb E_q[T(z)]+H(q)\}. \]

Inference balances agreement with the model against uncertainty. The first term rewards configurations favored by the model; entropy rewards spreading probability. Optimizing over every distribution recovers the target.

Let \(\mu=\mathbb E_q[T(z)]\), and let \(\mathcal M\) be the set of realizable moment vectors. The same optimization can be written

\[ A(\lambda)=\sup_{\mu\in\mathcal M} \{\lambda^\top\mu-A^\star(\mu)\}. \]

The convex conjugate \(A^\star\) is the negative maximum entropy compatible with those moments. This turns a problem about densities into one about feasible moments and entropy.

Where approximation enters becomes visible. Mean field restricts the distributions, hence the achievable moments, yielding a lower bound. Other methods relax difficult consistency constraints or approximate the entropy. Those changes do not all preserve the same bound or convergence properties.

As a simple example, two Bernoulli variables have moments \(\mu_1,\mu_2,\mu_{12}\), where \(\mu_{12}=\mathbb E[z_1z_2]\). Mean field imposes \(\mu_{12}=\mu_1\mu_2\). Even if the individual means are correct, this curved constraint excludes correlated joint distributions.

What this reading adds. It is a unifying mathematical monograph, not an extra algorithm to run. It explains why retaining dependence, changing entropy approximations, and relaxing a feasible set are distinct operations. The exact moment set can itself be computationally intractable, so the representation organizes the difficulty rather than removing it.

This geometry connects CAVI, Structured SVI, and EP without treating them as equivalent optimizers.

2015Estimator · Variational dropout

Variational Dropout and the Local Reparameterization Trick

The move. Sample local activations to reduce shared gradient noise, and develop a variational interpretation of dropout.

Read with AEVB · Stochastic backpropagation · Joint control variate · Bayes by Backprop · Sparse variational dropout

The idea and the mathematics

Reparameterization made a single draw differentiable, but it did not tell us where to inject the noise. In a Bayesian neural network, one random weight sample can influence every observation in a minibatch, giving their gradient contributions common noise. Local reparameterization asks whether we can sample the induced activations instead, retaining the needed marginals without sharing that noise.

The uncertain weights \(W\) are part of \(z\), with Gaussian parameters \((m,s)\) contained in \(\phi\).

If independent weights satisfy \(W_j\sim\mathcal N(m_j,s_j^2)\), a linear activation for input \(x_i\) satisfies

\[ A_i=\sum_j x_{ij}W_j \sim\mathcal N\!\left(\sum_jx_{ij}m_j,\sum_jx_{ij}^2s_j^2\right). \]

For a sum of expected per-observation log likelihoods, these marginal activation distributions suffice. Sample the activations independently across observations rather than sharing a weight draw across the whole minibatch. The expected gradient is preserved and the shared-noise covariance between observations is removed. Both AEVB and Local reparameterization use Gaussian reparameterization. What changes is where the noise is sampled.

Why a larger minibatch may fail to average away the noise. Let \(g_i\) be a scalar gradient contribution from observation \(i\), with variance \(v_g\) and common pairwise covariance \(c_g\). For a batch of size \(M=|\mathcal B|\),

\[ \operatorname{Var}\!\left(\frac1M\sum_i g_i\right) =\frac{v_g}{M}+\frac{M-1}{M}c_g. \]

One unusual weight draw can shift every observation's gradient in the same direction. Averaging many observations then leaves a common random fluctuation. Independent activation noise removes this shared source: each observation still has the correct activation distribution, but its sampling error can cancel against the others.

For example, doubling a scalar input doubles the activation's mean and multiplies its variance by four. We can sample that Gaussian activation directly without first sampling every contributing weight. The paper connects this efficient noise injection to a variational interpretation of dropout.

The key limitation is the objective's structure. Equality of marginal activation distributions preserves a sum of per-observation expectations. It does not preserve the joint law of predictions across inputs. If a calculation needs those correlations—such as joint posterior predictive draws—or couples observations inside one nonlinear term, independent local draws cannot simply replace a shared weight draw.

What carries forward. The principle generalizes whenever the objective is a sum of separate expected terms and we can sample their marginal intermediate quantities. It does not preserve joint predictions across observations, so it cannot replace shared-weight sampling for every calculation.

The Generalized Reparameterization Gradient

The move. Allow the transformed noise distribution to depend on the parameters, then correct for that dependence.

Read with AEVB · Acceptance–rejection gradients · Implicit reparameterization

The idea and the mathematics

The fixed-noise representation is elegant for Gaussians: subtract the mean, divide by the scale, and the remaining noise no longer depends on the parameters. Other distributions may admit only a partial standardization. Generalized reparameterization keeps the useful smooth path and explicitly pays for any dependence left in the standardized noise.

To retain both effects in the derivative, let \(z=T_\phi(\epsilon)\), but allow the transformed noise to have a density \(\nu_\phi\) that still depends on \(\phi\). For the transformed contribution \(\ell_\phi(\epsilon)=\log w_\phi(T_\phi(\epsilon))\),

\[ \nabla_\phi\mathbb E_{\nu_\phi}[\ell_\phi] =\mathbb E_{\nu_\phi}\!\left[\nabla_\phi \ell_\phi+\ell_\phi\nabla_\phi\log \nu_\phi\right]. \]

The first term differentiates through the transform. The second corrects for the movement of the noise distribution. The paper chooses transforms that reduce that dependence, for example by standardizing selected moments. AEVB is the case in which the second term vanishes identically. This trades some remaining score-function variance for a wider choice of distributions.

Think of standardization as removing as much motion as possible. For a Gaussian, subtracting the mean and dividing by the scale leaves a fixed standard normal. For other families, a similar transform may remove location and scale changes while the shape of the standardized distribution still changes. For a Gamma variable, standardizing its logarithm can remove its mean and variance dependence, but not generally all dependence on the shape parameter.

The derivative has to track both effects. The first term asks how the transformed sample moves when we hold its standardized value fixed. The second asks how the probability of seeing that standardized value changes. Dropping the latter would treat an almost-fixed noise law as if it were exactly fixed.

This is useful when a fully parameter-independent transform is inconvenient but a good partial standardization is available. The hope is that most of the useful derivative travels through the smoother pathwise term, leaving a smaller score correction.

Where it can struggle. Matching a few moments need not make the remaining distribution weakly dependent on its parameters. In strongly changing or extreme-shape regimes, the correction can still be noisy. The transformation is therefore part of the estimator design, not merely an algebraic rewrite. Implicit gradients offer another way to handle such families.

What carries forward. The reusable design rule is not 'always find perfect parameter-free noise'; it is 'move as much sensitivity as possible into a pathwise term, then correct the remainder.' The correction is what preserves the derivative.

Reparameterization Gradients through Acceptance-Rejection Sampling Algorithms

The move. Differentiate through an accepted proposal and correct for the distribution of the accepted noise.

Read with Generalized reparameterization · Implicit reparameterization

The idea and the mathematics

Generalized reparameterization tells us what happens when transformed noise is still parameter-dependent. A rejection sampler creates that exact situation for a less obvious reason: among all proposed noise values, only some are accepted, and the selection rule changes with the parameters.

Suppose a proposal is generated as \(z=T_\phi(\epsilon)\) and accepted through a probability depending on \(z\). Among accepted draws, the density of \(\epsilon\) is proportional to its proposal density times the acceptance probability. The accepted noise therefore generally depends on \(\phi\), even when the original proposal noise does not. Differentiate through \(T_\phi\) and add the correction from differentiating the accepted-noise density. This is the same decomposition as Generalized reparameterization, with the transform supplied by a sampler. It explains why simply backpropagating through the accepted proposal can miss part of the derivative.

The subtlety is selection. Imagine a parameter change that leaves a proposed value almost unchanged but makes it more likely to be accepted. Differentiating only the proposed value misses that change in which values reach the output. A rejection sampler is not a single smooth path: changing a parameter can change which proposal becomes the accepted one.

The paper avoids differentiating the entire sequence of accept/reject decisions. It instead describes the distribution of the noise after acceptance. Once that distribution is known, its derivative supplies the missing selection effect. The resulting estimator combines a derivative through the accepted proposal with a correction for the accepted-noise law.

When the proposal closely matches the variational distribution, acceptance is nearly uniform over proposal draws. The selection effect is then small, which explains why a good rejection sampler can also give a useful gradient estimator. The paper applies this idea to families such as Gamma and Dirichlet, for which practical samplers already use rejection constructions.

When it becomes costly. A poor proposal causes many rejections and a stronger correction; both computation and gradient noise can increase. Access to a sampler's output alone is insufficient: this derivation needs its proposal transformation and the quantities defining acceptance. Implicit gradients separate the derivative more directly from the sampler implementation.

What carries forward. The core idea is to differentiate the law of accepted randomness, not an accept/reject branch in a program. This can be reused whenever a proposal sampler has an accessible acceptance mechanism, but not from an opaque sample-only interface.

Implicit Reparameterization Gradients

The move. Differentiate the equation that defines a sample instead of differentiating an explicit sampling transform.

Read with Generalized reparameterization · Acceptance–rejection gradients · Pathwise transport

The idea and the mathematics

Acceptance-rejection gradients use details of a particular sampler. But what if we can sample from a distribution and evaluate its CDF, while its inverse is awkward or unavailable? The sample already satisfies a mathematical equation: its CDF value is a uniform percentile. Differentiate that equation rather than the code that generated the sample.

For a scalar distribution with CDF \(F_\phi\), write a draw as the solution to \(F_\phi(z)=u\), with \(u\) uniform. Differentiate at fixed \(u\):

\[ \partial_\phi z=-\frac{\partial_\phi F_\phi(z)}{q_\phi(z)}. \]

You need derivatives of the CDF at the sampled value. An explicit differentiable inverse CDF is unnecessary. The paper uses a more general implicit standardization formulation and applies it to distributions such as Gamma and Beta. Unlike Acceptance–rejection gradients’s derivation, the derivative here comes from a distributional identity that the sample satisfies, regardless of how the sample was generated.

Keep the percentile fixed, rather than the random-number program. If a draw lies at the 70th percentile, ask where the 70th percentile moves after a tiny parameter change. Differentiating the identity gives

\[ 0=\partial_\phi F_\phi(z)+ \underbrace{\partial_z F_\phi(z)}_{q_\phi(z)}\partial_\phi z. \]

The formula above follows by solving for the sample's velocity. If increasing a parameter raises the CDF at the current point, the fixed percentile must move left; that explains the minus sign. We never have to explicitly invert the CDF to obtain this derivative.

For a Gaussian, \(F_{m,s}(z)=\Phi((z-m)/s)\). The identity recovers \(\partial_m z=1\) and \(\partial_s z=(z-m)/s\), exactly the usual location-scale derivatives. Its value is that the same reasoning extends to distributions without such a simple explicit transform.

What must still be computed. We need accurate derivatives of the standardization function, often the CDF. Special-function derivatives and numerical behavior in the tails matter: dividing by a tiny density can be delicate. In several dimensions the corresponding standardization uses more structure, such as conditional CDFs; the scalar formula is an intuition, not a universal multivariate implementation.

What carries forward. Implicit differentiation separates the derivative of a draw from the algorithm that produced it. That distinction extends beyond VI wherever a continuous random value is characterized by a differentiable standardization relation.

Pathwise Derivatives Beyond the Reparameterization Trick

The move. Construct sample derivatives through probability-mass transport and compare their variance.

Read with Implicit reparameterization · SVGD

The idea and the mathematics

Implicit reparameterization gives a velocity for a one-dimensional sample. In multiple dimensions, however, the same change in a density can be realized by many different motions of probability mass. Pathwise transport takes this freedom seriously: if several velocities produce the correct derivative, some may be less noisy than others.

Treat \(v_\phi(z)\) as the velocity of probability mass as one component of \(\phi\) changes. If

\[ \partial_\phi q+\nabla_z\cdot(qv_\phi)=0, \]

then integration by parts gives \(\partial_\phi\mathbb E_q[f]=\mathbb E_q[\nabla f\cdot v_\phi]\) when the boundary term vanishes. This continuity equation explains why different ways of moving samples can produce the same expected derivative and different variances. In one dimension, writing \(F_\phi\) for the CDF, integrating the continuity equation gives \(qv_\phi=-\partial_\phi F_\phi\) with the usual boundary condition, recovering Implicit reparameterization.

A density change does not uniquely identify a motion of particles. Picture a two-dimensional cloud. A parameter change may spread it horizontally. Many individual particle motions can produce that same horizontal spreading; some also circulate mass around the cloud without changing the density. All valid motions must satisfy the continuity equation, which says that mass leaving one region arrives somewhere else.

Multiplying that equation by \(f\) and integrating moves the divergence onto \(f\):

\[ \partial_\phi\int f q\,dz =-\int f\,\nabla\!\cdot(qv_\phi)\,dz =\int q\,\nabla f\cdot v_\phi\,dz. \]

Thus the average derivative is fixed by the changing distribution, while individual sampled derivatives depend on the chosen velocity. The paper uses this freedom to study transport-based derivatives, including constructions for multivariate distributions.

Why this matters for variance. An unnecessary circulation can make individual values of \(\nabla f\cdot v_\phi\) large and opposing, even when their average is small. Choosing a better transport can reduce that cancellation. However, minimizing the size of a velocity field does not automatically minimize variance for every integrand; the directions in which \(f\) changes also matter. Constructing a useful field in high dimensions can be substantially harder than applying a familiar sampling transform.

What carries forward. This perspective exposes estimator design as a transport problem. The continuity equation is useful beyond Gaussian samplers, but a mathematically valid transport field is not necessarily cheap or variance-optimal for a particular integrand.

The Concrete Distribution: A Continuous Relaxation of Discrete Random Variables

The move. Replace a discrete draw with a smooth, temperature-controlled relaxation that supports pathwise gradients.

Read with AEVB · REBAR · RELAX

The idea and the mathematics

All the previous pathwise arguments rely on draws that can move smoothly. A categorical value cannot: a tiny parameter change usually leaves the sampled category unchanged, then occasionally flips it. The Concrete distribution creates a continuously moving substitute, so differentiation is possible—but now we must ask whether we're differentiating the original problem or a relaxed one.

A categorical draw can be generated by taking \(\arg\max_i(\ell_i+\epsilon_i)\), where \(\ell_i\) are logits (unnormalized log probabilities) and \(\epsilon_i\) are independent Gumbel draws. Replace the hard maximum with

\[ y_i=\operatorname{softmax}\!\big((\ell+\epsilon)/\tau\big)_i. \]

For \(\tau\gt 0\), \(y\) lies in the simplex and is differentiable in \(\ell\). As \(\tau\downarrow0\), it approaches the one-hot draw. Differentiating the relaxed objective generally gives a biased gradient for the original discrete objective. REBAR keeps the discrete objective and uses the relaxation inside a control variate.

Why soften a choice? A hard categorical sample stays on one vertex until a logit crosses a threshold; its derivative is zero almost everywhere. The relaxed sample moves through the simplex's interior. For three categories, a point such as \((0.8,0.15,0.05)\) expresses a soft choice that can move continuously toward a different vertex, allowing a downstream differentiable function to send a gradient back to the logits.

The temperature controls that movement. A high temperature gives smooth but less discrete samples. A low temperature places samples close to vertices, but the derivative

\[ \frac{\partial y_i}{\partial\ell_j} =\frac1\tau y_i(\mathbf1\{i=j\}-y_j) \]

reveals the difficulty: it is tiny for decisive choices and can be very large near a tie. Taking the temperature toward zero does not give an effortless discrete gradient.

When the relaxation is useful. The downstream computation must make sense on soft inputs. If fractional categories behave very differently from every actual category, the relaxed optimizer may exploit those interior states, creating a gap when we return to hard choices. Temperature selection trades this mismatch against gradient quality. REBAR uses the same smoothness to reduce variance while correcting back to the original discrete objective.

What carries forward. A relaxation can be used either as a new surrogate objective or as a component of an unbiased estimator. Concrete follows the first route; REBAR later illustrates the second. Keeping that distinction visible prevents a common confusion.

Neural Variational Inference and Learning in Belief Networks

The move. Train an inference network and generative model with variance-reduced score-function ELBO gradients.

Read with BBVI · MuProp · VIMCO

The idea and the mathematics

Concrete gives smooth gradients by changing the sampled object. But what if we want the original discrete objective unchanged? NVIL returns to the score estimator: rather than smoothing the discrete choice, it subtracts a baseline predicting the part of its learning signal that is already explained by the observation.

NVIL uses a score estimator of the form \((\log w-b(x))\nabla_\phi\log q_\phi(z\mid x)\), with a learned input-dependent baseline and other variance-reduction measures. For fixed \(x\), the conditional expectation of the score is zero, so subtracting \(b(x)\) preserves the expected gradient. BBVI already uses the score as a control variate. NVIL makes the baseline depend on the observation whose discrete latent variables are being inferred.

Credit should be relative to the observation. A difficult observation may have a low log weight for almost every latent configuration, while an easy one has a high log weight. That difference can dominate the score estimator even though the useful question is which latent configuration explains this observation better. An input-dependent baseline learns the predictable part of the learning signal before seeing the random latent draw.

For a Bernoulli latent variable with probability \(p=\operatorname{sigmoid}(\phi)\), the score is \(z-p\). The update is proportional to \((\log w-b(x))(z-p)\): a better-than-baseline draw encourages the probability of the sampled choice, while a worse one discourages it. Centering the signal makes that comparison easier to estimate.

NVIL also uses variance normalization and more local learning signals for multilayer models. These choices turn a very general but noisy score identity into a practical way to train discrete belief networks with an inference network.

What a baseline cannot explain. It can predict difficulty from \(x\), but it cannot distinguish two draws for the same \(x\) if it never sees \(z\). Strong variation between latent configurations remains. Giving a baseline arbitrary dependence on the draw would lose the simple zero-mean argument; MuProp and REBAR introduce the corrections needed to use richer, sample-dependent controls.

What carries forward. An input-dependent baseline is broadly reusable whenever the score has conditional mean zero. It reduces avoidable variation without altering the expected gradient, but cannot by itself explain differences among latent draws for the same observation.

MuProp: Unbiased Backpropagation for Stochastic Neural Networks

The move. Subtract a linear surrogate from the noisy score signal and add back its exactly computed gradient.

Read with NVIL · REBAR · Taylor controls

The idea and the mathematics

NVIL's baseline sees the observation but not the sampled latent value. That means it can remove a shared offset, yet much of the draw-to-draw variation remains. MuProp builds a predictor that does depend on the draw: a local linear approximation to the cost. It then adds back the predictor's expected gradient exactly.

For a discrete variable \(z\), let \(m=m_\phi=\mathbb E_{q_\phi}[z]\) and approximate \(f(z)\) with its first-order expansion \(\tilde f(z)=f(m)+\nabla f(m)^\top(z-m)\). Treat the reference point and Taylor coefficients as fixed when forming the correction at the current parameter value. Apply the score estimator to \(f(z)-\tilde f(z)\), then add \(\nabla f(m)^\top\nabla_\phi m_\phi\). This add-back is the exact score expectation of the linear term. Where NVIL uses an input-dependent constant, MuProp subtracts a function of the actual draw. The add-back is what makes that possible without introducing bias.

Separate the predictable slope from the surprise. A constant baseline says roughly how large \(f\) will be. A linear approximation also predicts how \(f\) changes when the sample moves away from its mean. MuProp sends this predictable linear change through ordinary differentiation and asks the score estimator to handle only the residual:

\[ \widehat g_\phi= [f(z)-\tilde f(z)]S_\phi(z) +\nabla f(m)^\top\nabla_\phi m_\phi. \]

For \(f(z)=az+b\), the Taylor approximation is exact. The random residual is zero, and the derivative is simply \(a\nabla_\phi m_\phi\). This toy example explains the benefit: if the function is nearly linear over the sampled region, little is left for Monte Carlo to estimate.

In a multilayer stochastic network, the paper constructs the reference using a deterministic pass that propagates means. This is a reference computation for the control variate; it is not a replacement of the original random network by its mean.

When it struggles. A sharp nonlinear interaction may be poorly represented by a tangent at the mean, especially if typical discrete configurations lie far from that mean. Evaluating \(f\) and its derivative at the reference must also make sense. For an integrand with explicit parameter dependence, its direct derivative is included as well; the formula here isolates the gradient arising from the sampling distribution.

What carries forward. The general recipe is subtract a cheap surrogate, estimate the residual, add back an analytic expectation. MuProp applies this to discrete stochastic networks; later Taylor and quadratic controls reuse the same logic for pathwise gradients.

REBAR: Low-variance, unbiased gradient estimates for discrete latent variable models

The move. Use a continuous relaxation as a control variate, with a correction that preserves the discrete objective.

Read with Concrete · MuProp · RELAX

The idea and the mathematics

A smooth relaxation is a useful predictor of a discrete cost, but optimizing the relaxation directly changes the question. REBAR combines the two ideas we've just encountered: use the relaxation as a sample-dependent control, then correct its mean contribution so that the estimator still differentiates the original discrete objective.

Let \(u\) be a continuous variable from which a hard transformation produces the discrete sample \(z\). Draw \(\widetilde u\) from the conditional law of \(u\) given \(z\). For a differentiable relaxed function \(c\), the estimator has the form

\[ [f(z)-c(\widetilde u)]\,\nabla_\phi\log q_\phi(z) +\nabla_\phi c(u)-\nabla_\phi c(\widetilde u), \]

where the last two derivatives follow the unconditional and conditional sampling transforms, respectively, with \(z\) held fixed in the conditional derivative. Why does this work? Decompose \(\mathbb E[c(u)]\) by conditioning on \(z\), then differentiate it in two ways. The difference of the two pathwise terms cancels the expected baseline-score term. Concrete supplies the relaxation. REBAR supplies the correction that preserves the discrete gradient.

Use a soft version as a predictor, not as the problem to solve. If the relaxed value closely tracks the discrete function value, \(f(z)-c(\widetilde u)\) fluctuates much less than \(f(z)\). That makes the score term quieter. But because this predictor depends on the discrete sample, it is no longer an ordinary constant baseline. The two pathwise terms pay back exactly the mean that was subtracted.

The conditional draw is essential. It produces a soft sample consistent with the hard choice already made. For a binary threshold, the underlying uniform must be drawn within the interval that produces that outcome. This couples the soft and hard computations so that their fluctuations track one another.

The important change from Concrete is therefore where the relaxation is used: REBAR still estimates the gradient of the discrete objective. Its temperature affects variance, rather than introducing relaxation bias into that objective.

The cost of this correction. We need unconditional and conditional relaxed samples, additional function evaluations, and derivatives through the relaxed computations. A poor surrogate or an extreme temperature can leave a noisy residual or large pathwise corrections. The method is attractive when the relaxation predicts the discrete function well enough to justify those extra computations. RELAX learns a more adaptable predictor.

What carries forward. The reusable contribution is an unbiased conditional-relaxation control variate. The relaxed function need not define the target loss; it can be used solely to cancel noise. The price is conditional sampling and extra derivatives.

Backpropagation through the Void: Optimizing control variates for black-box gradient estimation

The move. Learn the control variate itself by minimizing the variance of an unbiased gradient estimator.

Read with REBAR · ARM

The idea and the mathematics

REBAR's control follows a particular relaxed version of the discrete cost. That may be a poor predictor when the true cost is complicated. RELAX asks whether we can learn the control itself for the sole purpose of reducing gradient variance, without requiring it to be a faithful model of the cost everywhere.

RELAX uses the same unconditional-minus-conditional correction as REBAR, with a learned differentiable function \(c_\psi\). If \(\widehat g_{\phi,\psi}\) is an unbiased estimator for each fixed \(\psi\), its mean does not depend on \(\psi\), so

\[ \nabla_\psi\operatorname{Var}(\widehat g_{\phi,\psi})=\nabla_\psi\mathbb E[\widehat g_{\phi,\psi}^2] \]

for a scalar component. This gives a training objective for the control variate itself. REBAR starts from a relaxation of the original function. RELAX allows a learned function whose job is to make the gradient estimator less noisy.

The auxiliary network learns how to cancel noise. A good control need not reproduce the function value everywhere. Its purpose is to fluctuate with the original estimator so that subtraction removes noise. This differs from learning the posterior or learning the generative model: \(\psi\) controls estimator quality, while \(\phi\) controls the approximation being fitted.

For a vector gradient, the corresponding training target is its squared norm:

\[ \nabla_\psi\operatorname{tr}\operatorname{Cov}(\widehat g_{\phi,\psi}) =\nabla_\psi\mathbb E[\|\widehat g_{\phi,\psi}\|^2]. \]

The mean-gradient term disappears because the correction preserves it for every fixed choice of \(\psi\). We can therefore update the control using a sampled gradient, then continue updating the inference parameters with the corrected estimator. The paper also develops LAX for continuous variables; RELAX adds the conditional construction needed for discrete choices.

Why more flexibility is not free. A learned control can fit nonlinear patterns that a fixed relaxation misses, but it needs its own optimization and derivatives through the gradient estimator. Early in training it may be a poor predictor, and its extra evaluations can outweigh a variance reduction on cheap problems. The useful comparison is progress per unit of computation, not just the variance of one update. Simple paired estimators such as ARM avoid training this auxiliary network.

What carries forward. The general principle is to learn an auxiliary object against estimator variance, not model fit. Its parameters serve the optimizer, not the posterior. This separation is useful across stochastic optimization, although the training and evaluation cost may outweigh the gain.

ARM: Augment-REINFORCE-Merge Gradient for Stochastic Binary Networks

The move. Pair binary samples through shared uniforms so their function evaluations cancel noise.

Read with REBAR · DisARM

The idea and the mathematics

Learned controls can be powerful, but they introduce another network to train. For Bernoulli variables we can exploit more specific structure instead: there are only two possible values per coordinate. ARM constructs two coupled binary evaluations from one uniform draw so that much of the irrelevant fluctuation cancels in their difference.

For one binary unknown, write \(z\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(\operatorname{sigmoid}(\phi))\), where \(\operatorname{sigmoid}(a)=1/(1+e^{-a})\) converts a real-valued logit into a probability. The exact derivative is

\[ \frac{d}{d\phi}\mathbb E_{q_\phi}[f(z)] =\operatorname{sigmoid}(\phi)(1-\operatorname{sigmoid}(\phi))[f(1)-f(0)]. \]

With many binary unknowns, enumerating configurations is expensive. ARM instead draws one uniform vector \(u\) and constructs two related binary vectors:

\[ z^+=\mathbf1\{u\gt \operatorname{sigmoid}(-\phi)\},\qquad z^-=\mathbf1\{u\lt \operatorname{sigmoid}(\phi)\}. \]
\[ \widehat g_\phi=(f(z^+)-f(z^-))(u-\tfrac12), \qquad u\sim\mathrm{Uniform}([0,1]^d). \]

The thresholds act coordinatewise. Both evaluations share the same noise, and their difference cancels part of its effect. This antithetic pairing gives an unbiased derivative for a fixed function \(f\) without a differentiable relaxation.

REBAR uses a smooth surrogate to reduce noise. ARM evaluates only binary configurations. DisARM further averages out randomness left in the uniform variables once the paired outcomes are known.

At probability one half, the cancellation is visible. In the scalar case with \(\phi=0\), one uniform draw below \(1/2\) produces \((z^+,z^-)=(0,1)\), and a draw above \(1/2\) produces \((1,0)\). Writing \(\Delta f=f(1)-f(0)\), both cases give

\[ \widehat g_\phi=\Delta f\,|u-\tfrac12|, \qquad \mathbb E[\widehat g_\phi]=\tfrac14\Delta f. \]

This is the exact Bernoulli derivative in expectation. Adding a constant to \(f\) changes neither paired difference nor estimator. A raw score estimator, by contrast, can become noisier under that same irrelevant offset.

For many binary coordinates, evaluating every configuration would require exponentially many function calls. ARM uses two coupled configurations to estimate the whole gradient vector. The shared uniforms make this much more informative than comparing two unrelated function evaluations.

Its practical boundary. Two function calls may be expensive, and a binary factorization is central to this construction. Interactions among many bits can still make the paired differences noisy. If \(f\) itself depends explicitly on \(\phi\), that direct derivative must also be included. DisARM retains these paired evaluations while eliminating randomness in the uniforms that is unnecessary once the outcomes are known.

What carries forward. Antithetic coupling is the reusable idea. ARM's simple formula relies on Bernoulli logits; the general strategy of coupling two function evaluations extends further, but the exact identity does not automatically extend to arbitrary discrete variables.

DisARM: An Antithetic Gradient Estimator for Binary Latent Variables

The move. Average out extra randomness in an antithetic binary estimator while keeping the paired evaluations.

Read with ARM · BBVI

The idea and the mathematics

ARM already couples its two binary samples. Yet after the two binary outcomes have been determined, the exact uniform numbers used to create them still vary. DisARM notices that this leftover variation can be integrated out analytically, preserving the same mean with no extra function evaluations.

The paired discrete outcomes in ARM usually leave some uncertainty about the uniforms that produced them. DisARM analytically computes \(\mathbb E[\widehat g_{\phi,\mathrm{ARM}}\mid z^+,z^-]\). By total variance,

\[ \operatorname{Var}\big(\mathbb E[\widehat g_{\phi,\mathrm{ARM}}\mid z^+,z^-]\big) \leq \operatorname{Var}(\widehat g_{\phi,\mathrm{ARM}}). \]

This is a direct improvement to ARM by Rao–Blackwellization. It keeps the paired function evaluations and integrates out the unnecessary randomness between the thresholds.

Return to ARM's fair-coin example. When \(p=1/2\), knowing which of the two outcomes occurred tells us whether \(u\) lies in the lower or upper half of the unit interval. ARM's value still depends on the precise location within that half. But its conditional average is already known:

\[ \mathbb E[|u-\tfrac12|\mid z^+,z^-]=\tfrac14. \]

For one bit and a fixed \(f\), DisARM therefore returns the exact derivative \(\tfrac14[f(1)-f(0)]\) in this example. The function values are unchanged; we have integrated out a random multiplier that was no longer needed.

More generally, the paper derives the conditional expectations associated with the paired binary outcomes. Total variance splits ARM's variability into the variability of these conditional means and the remaining variability within each condition. DisARM removes the second part, explaining the variance improvement directly rather than relying only on an empirical comparison.

What remains random. In a multivariate problem, the paired configurations still vary, and a nonlinear \(f\) can assign them very different values. Conditioning does not enumerate all configurations or solve their interactions. The gain is relative to the corresponding ARM construction; it does not establish that DisARM is cheaper or lower-variance than every learned control-variate method on every model.

What carries forward. This is Rao–Blackwellization applied to an estimator rather than to a posterior approximation. Whenever an estimator contains auxiliary randomness that can be conditioned away exactly, this is a general route to variance reduction.

Sticking the Landing: Simple, Lower-Variance Gradient Estimators for Variational Inference

The move. Remove the explicit zero-mean score term from the pathwise ELBO gradient.

Read with AEVB · Taylor controls · DReG · STL convergence · Kernelized path gradients

The idea and the mathematics

Variance reduction is not only a discrete-variable problem. Even for a smooth Gaussian draw, the ordinary pathwise ELBO derivative contains a term whose expectation is exactly zero. Sticking the Landing removes it, revealing that at an exact posterior fit the remaining per-sample gradient can be identically zero.

Write \(z_\phi=T_\phi(\epsilon)\), with \(\epsilon\sim\nu\). Expand the total derivative in AEVB:

\[ \frac{d}{d\phi}\log w_\phi(z_\phi) =\nabla_z\log w_\phi(z_\phi)\,\partial_\phi z_\phi -\left.\partial_\phi\log q_\phi(z)\right|_{z=z_\phi}. \]

The second term is a zero-mean score. Drop it and keep the first. If \(q_\phi=\pi\), then \(\log w_\phi(z)=\log Z\) is constant in \(z\), so the remaining estimator vanishes for every draw. This gives the zero-variance property at an exact posterior fit. In BBVI the score helps construct the gradient. Here it is a dispensable term in a pathwise gradient. The same deletion needs extra care for IWAE’s multisample objective, which motivates DReG.

An exact fit should stop producing random pushes. Take a standard normal target and \(q_\phi=\mathcal N(m,s^2)\), with \(z=m+s\epsilon\). For the derivative with respect to \(m\), the ordinary total derivative and STL estimator are

\[ \widehat g_m^{\mathrm{total}}=-m-s\epsilon, \qquad \widehat g_m^{\mathrm{STL}}=-m+(s^{-1}-s)\epsilon. \]

Both have mean \(-m\). When \(s=1\), the STL estimator is exactly \(-m\) for every draw; at \(m=0\), it is identically zero. The total derivative still contains the random term \(-\epsilon\), even though the posterior fit is exact.

The benefit depends on the fit. The same example shows why removal of a zero-mean term does not always reduce variance: if \(s\) is very small, \(s^{-1}-s\) can be large. The zero-variance property is particularly compelling near a well-specified optimum. If the family cannot represent the posterior, residual noise can remain, a distinction developed in STL convergence.

In an autodiff implementation, the explicit parameters inside the log-density evaluation are held fixed for the score deletion, while differentiation through the sampled \(z\) is preserved. Stopping the entire log-density derivative would remove a necessary part of the pathwise term as well.

What carries forward. This result is an estimator identity, not a new ELBO or family. Crucially, a zero-mean term need not always be beneficial to remove away from the optimum. The strong claim is pointwise cancellation at a well-specified fit, which later convergence papers exploit.

The idea and the mathematics

STL removes a zero-mean term already hidden inside the gradient. What if the remaining pathwise gradient is still noisy? We can predict part of that noise using a local Taylor approximation, subtract the prediction on each draw, and add back its expectation without Monte Carlo.

Use the pathwise gradient of a Taylor approximation as a correlated random quantity \(C\). Then use \(\widehat g_\phi-C+\mathbb E[C]\), where \(\mathbb E[C]\) is available from known moments of the base noise. The idea and the mathematics

The previous controls mainly address randomness from draws of latent variables. Yet a modern VI gradient may also vary because it is computed from a random minibatch. \[ \widehat g_\phi(i,\epsilon)-\widetilde g_{\phi^{(\tau_i)}}(i,\epsilon) +\mathbb E_{j,\epsilon^\prime}[\widetilde g_{\phi^{(\tau_j)}}(j,\epsilon^\prime)]. \]

Here \(\tau_i\) is the iteration at which the stored parameters for observation \(i\) were last refreshed; \(j\) and \(\epsilon^\prime\) are fresh data and noise draws in the add-back expectation.

The sampled surrogate sees the same index and the same noise as the original gradient. A table of stored parameters and a running average make the add-back tractable. \[ \operatorname{Var}(\widehat g_\phi) =\operatorname{Var}_i\!\left(\mathbb E_\epsilon[\widehat g_\phi\mid i]\right) +\mathbb E_i\!\left[\operatorname{Var}_\epsilon(\widehat g_\phi\mid i)\right]. \]

Reducing only the second term eventually hits a floor set by the first. The joint control subtracts a surrogate carrying both the selected observation's typical contribution and its response to the particular noise draw. The add-back restores the average over all observations and noise.

The idea and the mathematics

So far, most methods have left the ELBO unchanged and worked on its optimization. But the objective itself is also a modeling choice. \[ \nabla\log\!\left(\frac1K\sum_s w_s\right) =\sum_s\bar w_s\nabla\log w_s. \]

If one weight dominates, the effective contribution of the other samples is small. And although the objective becomes a tighter bound, it also becomes less sensitive to the inference parameters as it approaches \(\log Z\). Tighter is not always better separates these two effects; DReG improves the inference-gradient estimator. A larger \(K\) changes both the statistical objective and the computation needed to train it.

Operator Variational Inference

The move. Fit an approximation by testing identities satisfied by the target, including objectives that need samples but no variational density.

Read with Rényi VI · SVGD · Adversarial VI · Kernel SIVI

The idea and the mathematics

The ELBO is one way to turn inference into optimization. Operator VI asks how to construct other objectives, including ones that can fit a sampler whose density is unavailable.

Start with a property that the target must satisfy. For a differentiable target on \(\mathbb R^d\), define the Langevin–Stein operator

\[ \mathcal A_\pi f(z) =\nabla_z\log\gamma(z)^\top f(z)+\nabla_z\cdot f(z). \]

Here \(f\) is a vector-valued test function. With suitable boundary behavior, integration by parts gives \(\mathbb E_\pi[\mathcal A_\pi f]=0\). The target score uses \(\gamma\), since differentiating removes \(\log Z\).

Turn a target identity into a test of the approximation. If samples from \(q_\phi\) consistently violate the identity, they reveal a mismatch. One operator objective is

\[ \mathcal D_{\mathcal F}(q_\phi,\pi) =\sup_{f\in\mathcal F} \left(\mathbb E_{q_\phi}[\mathcal A_\pi f(z)]\right)^2. \]

The chosen test class \(\mathcal F\) controls which discrepancies are visible and must make the optimization well posed. A critic searches for a revealing test; the sampler adjusts to reduce its violation.

Consider a standard normal target in one dimension. Its operator is \(\mathcal A_\pi f(z)=-zf(z)+f'(z)\). The test \(f(z)=1\) detects a nonzero mean; \(f(z)=z\) detects a second moment different from one. These two tests alone cannot distinguish a Gaussian from every other distribution with matching moments. Richer tests can expose discrepancies they miss.

What changes. This example replaces reverse KL with a discrepancy built from target identities. It needs target derivatives and variational samples, but no evaluation of \(\log q_\phi\). The paper's broader framework specifies an operator, a test family, and a distance from zero.

It is useful for implicit variational programs. Weak critics can overlook errors, and joint critic–sampler optimization can be difficult. SVGD uses a related Stein identity for particle transport; adversarial VI instead learns a density ratio to retain an ELBO-based objective.

Filtering Variational Objectives

The move. Build the lower bound from a particle filter's estimate of the normalizing constant.

Read with IWAE · MCMC–VI bridge

The idea and the mathematics

The previous alternative bounds treat samples as complete latent explanations. For a long hidden-state sequence, that can be wasteful: we may spend computation extending a trajectory that failed to explain observations long ago. FIVO instead feeds a particle-filter estimate of the evidence into the log-bound construction, exploiting the sequential model structure.

Any positive unbiased estimator \(\widehat Z\) of \(Z\) gives \(\mathbb E[\log\widehat Z]\leq\log Z\). IWAE uses importance sampling to construct \(\widehat Z\). This paper uses a particle filter, whose likelihood estimate is a product of average incremental weights across time, with particles resampled along the way. The filter can preserve useful partial trajectories instead of weighting entire independent trajectories only at the end. That changes the likelihood estimator inside the bound. It also introduces discrete resampling decisions that matter when differentiating the objective.

Long sequences make one-shot importance sampling fragile. A trajectory can explain the first few observations well and then fail later; another can waste most of its probability on a poor early choice. Weighting complete trajectories only at the end spends computation extending many paths that were already implausible.

A particle filter revisits this allocation along the sequence. It extends partial trajectories, scores them against the next observation, and resamples promising paths. The resulting likelihood estimator can have much lower relative variance than ordinary trajectory importance sampling, which can make its expected logarithm a more useful bound.

The connection between noise and the bound is visible in a local expansion. If \(\widehat Z=Z+e\) is concentrated near \(Z\) and \(\mathbb E[e]=0\), then

\[ \mathbb E[\log\widehat Z] \approx\log Z-\frac{\operatorname{Var}(\widehat Z)}{2Z^2}. \]

This is an intuition for why a better likelihood estimator can improve a variational objective; it is not a universal variance-only ordering of all estimators.

The new difficulty is resampling. Its discrete choices affect the gradient. The paper discusses their high-variance contributions and omits some of them in practical training, trading unbiasedness for a more usable estimator. Too few particles can also lose diverse ancestral paths. FIVO is especially compelling for sequential structure, but particle filtering does not remove proposal-design or gradient-estimation problems.

What carries forward. The reusable bridge is: a positive unbiased normalizer estimator yields a Jensen lower bound. The choice of estimator—independent importance sampling or sequential particle filtering—changes the bound and creates different gradient difficulties.

2016Gradient estimator

Variational Inference for Monte Carlo Objectives

The move. Give each importance sample a baseline computed from the other samples.

Read with NVIL · IWAE · VIMCO-star

The idea and the mathematics

IWAE gives a multisample objective, but a discrete inference network still needs a usable gradient of it. A single learning signal for the whole set does not indicate which sample helped. VIMCO gives each sample a counterfactual baseline built only from the other samples, so credit can be assigned without learning a separate baseline network.

For \(K\) independent draws \(z^{(s)}\sim q_\phi\), let \(w_s=w_\phi(z^{(s)})\). Write \(\ell=\log(K^{-1}\sum_{s=1}^K w_s)\) and \(\bar w_s=w_s/\sum_{r=1}^Kw_r\). With \(\phi\) appearing only in \(q_\phi\), the score-form gradient has contributions \((\ell-\bar w_s)S_\phi(z^{(s)})\). Write \(z^{(-s)}\) for all draws except \(z^{(s)}\). VIMCO subtracts a separate baseline \(b_s(z^{(-s)})\) from \(\ell\) in each contribution. A standard construction replaces \(w_s\) in the baseline by the geometric mean of the other weights. Because \(b_s\) uses only the other draws, \(\mathbb E[b_s S_\phi(z^{(s)})]=0\). This is the multisample version of the baseline idea in NVIL. VIMCO-star revisits the choice of replacement.

Several samples create a credit-assignment problem. The log average weight gives the same overall learning signal to every draw. If one draw is excellent and the rest poor, that shared number alone does not tell the encoder which draw deserves credit. VIMCO compares the actual set against a counterfactual set in which one weight is replaced using only its companions.

For \(K\gt 1\), the geometric replacement is

\[ \widetilde w_s=\exp\!\left(\frac1{K-1}\sum_{r\ne s}\log w_r\right), \qquad b_s=\log\!\left(\frac{\sum_{r\ne s}w_r+\widetilde w_s}{K}\right). \]

Then \(\ell-b_s\) measures how much the actual draw changed the set's log average relative to this reference. The additional \(-\bar w_s\) term accounts for the explicit dependence of \(w_s=\gamma/q_\phi\) on the inference parameters. The other draws supply the baseline without requiring a separately trained network.

When it helps. It is designed for multisample objectives with discrete latents, where pathwise gradients may be unavailable. It requires several draws, and highly unequal weights can still make credit assignment difficult. Moreover, reducing variance at one sample count does not guarantee favorable scaling as \(K\) grows: VIMCO-star shows why the particular replacement choice matters asymptotically.

What carries forward. The leave-one-out independence argument is widely reusable for multisample score estimators. The baseline may depend richly on other samples, but must not depend on the particular draw whose score it multiplies.

2018Analysis · Training objectives

Tighter Variational Bounds are Not Necessarily Better

The move. Explain the inference-gradient cost of tighter bounds and propose alternative allocations of training effort.

Read with IWAE · DReG · VIMCO-star · DGP gradient signal

The idea and the mathematics

It is tempting to think a bound closer to the true log evidence must provide better training. But in the limit of infinitely many importance samples, the bound loses its dependence on the inference network. This paper makes the resulting signal-to-noise problem precise and separates the gradient for the model from the gradient for the encoder.

For a scalar component \(\widehat g_\phi\) of an inference-gradient estimator, define \(\mathrm{SNR}=|\mathbb E[\widehat g_\phi]|/\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(\widehat g_\phi)}\). Under the paper’s regularity and nondegeneracy conditions, increasing \(K\) in standard IWAE gives model-parameter gradient SNR of order \(\sqrt K\), but inference-network gradient SNR of order \(K^{-1/2}\). The reason is that the limiting objective is \(\log p_\theta(x)\): it depends on the model parameters \(\theta\), but not on the inference parameters \(\phi\). As \(K\) grows, the objective becomes less sensitive to \(\phi\); the standard estimator’s noise does not shrink fast enough to compensate. DReG improves the estimator while retaining the objective.

A quieter estimate can still be a worse learning signal. In the asymptotic regime analyzed for the usual IWAE inference gradient, the mean shrinks on the order of \(K^{-1}\), while its standard deviation shrinks only on the order of \(K^{-1/2}\). With four times as many draws, the noise is roughly halved, but the signal is roughly quartered. Their ratio gets worse.

Why should the signal shrink? Importance sampling becomes less dependent on the proposal as its sample average becomes more accurate. In the infinite-sample limit, any suitable proposal recovers the same evidence. That is desirable for evidence estimation, but it removes the objective's reason to prefer one encoder over another.

The paper also distinguishes increasing the number of samples inside each log average from averaging more independent gradient estimates of a fixed-\(K\) objective. The latter reduces noise without flattening the objective in the same way. It studies ways of allocating computation and separating the objectives used to train the generative and inference networks.

How to interpret the result. It concerns a particular estimator and its large-\(K\) behavior, not a claim that every tighter bound trains worse or that smaller \(K\) always wins. DReG is an instructive counterpoint: the same IWAE objective can have a substantially better gradient estimator. Bound quality and estimator quality are separate design choices.

What carries forward. The general lesson is to distinguish objective tightness from gradient-estimator quality. More computation inside one log average and more independent replications of a gradient estimator have different effects.

Doubly Reparameterized Gradient Estimators for Monte Carlo Objectives

The move. Reparameterize the weighted score contribution again, producing squared normalized importance weights.

Read with Sticking the Landing · IWAE · Tighter bounds · VIMCO-star · Importance-weighted DGPs · DGP gradient signal

The idea and the mathematics

Rainforth et al. identify a problem with the standard inference gradient of IWAE. Is the objective responsible, or are we using an inefficient estimator? DReG keeps exactly the same multisample objective and reexpresses the weighted score contribution through a second pathwise identity.

Use \(z^{(s)}=T_\phi(\epsilon^{(s)})\), \(w_s=w_\phi(z^{(s)})\), and \(\bar w_s=w_s/\sum_{r=1}^K w_r\).

In a reparameterized IWAE gradient, the explicit score term is multiplied by the random normalized weight \(\bar w_s\). This product generally has nonzero expectation, so the deletion used in Sticking the Landing no longer applies. DReG instead rewrites the expectation of that score term as a pathwise derivative, yielding

\[ \nabla_\phi\mathcal L_K =\mathbb E\!\left[\sum_{s=1}^K\bar w_s^2\, \nabla_{z^{(s)}}\log w_s\,\partial_\phi z^{(s)}\right]. \]

The squared normalized weights are the visible result of the second reparameterization. At \(K=1\), \(\bar w_1=1\) and the formula reduces to Sticking the Landing. VIMCO-star pursues an analogous SNR improvement for cases where a pathwise derivative is unavailable.

The weighted score is the obstacle. An unweighted score averages to zero, but a sample with a large importance weight is not a typical unweighted draw. Multiplying its score by that weight changes the expectation. DReG applies a second reparameterization identity to account for this dependence, and combines the resulting terms rather than throwing one away.

The squared weights give an intuition for the scaling. If the weights are reasonably balanced, \(\bar w_s\) is approximately \(1/K\), so the \(K\) terms each carry a coefficient of about \(1/K^2\). The mean inference signal is still small, but the estimator now cancels more of the noise that obscured it. Under the paper's asymptotic assumptions, its inference-gradient SNR improves as \(\sqrt K\).

At an exact posterior fit, every \(w_s=Z\) and every spatial derivative \(\nabla_z\log w_s=0\). The estimator is then zero for each sampled set, extending STL's pointwise cancellation.

When the construction applies. It needs differentiable reparameterized draws and the appropriate moments for the SNR analysis. Discrete latents require another route, such as VIMCO-star. A single dominating weight also limits the benefit of averaging. In code, the displayed formula specifies an estimator directly; blindly differentiating a surrogate with squared weights can introduce unwanted derivatives unless its weights and density parameters are held fixed in the intended places.

What carries forward. This is a clean example of improving optimization without changing the target, family, or objective. The squared normalized weights reflect a valid derivative identity, not a heuristic deletion of terms as in a naive multisample STL.

Importance Weighted Variational Inference without the Reparameterization Trick

The move. Change the leave-one-out baseline to prevent signal-to-noise collapse in importance-weighted score gradients.

Read with VIMCO · Rényi VI · Tighter bounds · DReG

The idea and the mathematics

DReG repairs inference-gradient scaling when samples can move differentiably. What if they are discrete or otherwise non-reparameterizable? VIMCO-star revisits the leave-one-out score baseline and shows how a different reference can cancel the leading noisy term in the multisample gradient.

As in IWAE, \(w_s=w_\phi(z^{(s)})\) for independent draws \(z^{(s)}\sim q_\phi\).

In ordinary IWAE, VIMCO’s per-sample baseline has the form \(b_s=\log[K^{-1}(\sum_{r\ne s}w_r+\tilde w_s)]\). Standard variants fill the missing slot with an arithmetic or geometric mean of the other weights. For ordinary IWAE, the proposed VIMCO-\(\star\) takes that replacement to be zero. The baseline still excludes \(z^{(s)}\), so its product with the \(s\)-th score still has zero expectation. Under the preprint’s moment and nondegeneracy conditions, the inference-parameter gradient has mean of order \(K^{-1}\), while the new variance is of order \(K^{-3}\). Hence its SNR grows like \(\sqrt K\), compared with \(K^{-1/2}\) for the standard variants analyzed there. The closest comparison is DReG. Both address weak inference gradients for importance weighting. DReG uses a second pathwise identity, while VIMCO-\(\star\) improves a score-function estimator through the baseline.

Why replacing a missing weight by zero can help. For the ordinary IWAE case, let \(W=\sum_r w_r\) and \(a_s=w_s/W=\bar w_s\). The zero-replacement baseline gives

\[ \ell-b_s=\log\frac{W}{W-w_s}=-\log(1-a_s). \]

After including the explicit weight derivative, the score coefficient becomes

\[ \ell-b_s-a_s =-\log(1-a_s)-a_s =\tfrac12a_s^2+\tfrac13a_s^3+\cdots. \]

The first-order term cancels. When weights are balanced, \(a_s\) is of order \(1/K\), so this cancellation removes a leading source of fluctuations. It explains why the baseline choice matters beyond the generic statement that any leave-one-out baseline is unbiased.

This parallels DReG's squared-weight structure, but reaches it through score-function credit assignment rather than differentiating sample paths. The preprint treats the broader Rényi importance-weighted setting too; the zero-replacement explanation here is specifically for ordinary IWAE.

Where the intuition weakens. We need \(K\gt 1\) and a positive remaining weight sum. If one sample carries almost all the weight, \(a_s\) approaches one and \(-\log(1-a_s)\) becomes large; the small-weight expansion no longer predicts benign behavior. The stated SNR scaling relies on the preprint's moment conditions and a nonzero leading signal. It is an asymptotic result, rather than a guarantee for every small batch or heavy-tailed proposal.

This note follows the February 2026 preprint (v1). The October revision has a new title and extends the control-variate analysis.

What carries forward. The transferable idea is asymptotic cancellation through careful baseline design. Its specific rate statements belong to the stated moment assumptions and the identified preprint version, not every heavy-tailed or finite-sample regime.

Variational Inference with Normalizing Flows

The move. Deform a simple distribution with invertible maps while tracking its density through Jacobian determinants.

Read with AEVB · IAF · Hierarchical VI · Variational boosting · Adversarial VI · Copula VI · Gaussian copula VI

The idea and the mathematics

Better gradients cannot make a diagonal Gaussian represent a curved or multimodal posterior. Normalizing flows change a different piece of the problem: the set of distributions available to approximate the target. By transforming a simple draw and tracking the change in density, they retain an evaluable ELBO while fitting richer shapes.

Draw \(z_0\sim q_{\phi,0}\) and set \(z_\ell=T_{\phi,\ell}(z_{\ell-1})\) for invertible maps \(T_{\phi,\ell}\). Here \(\ell=1,\ldots,L\) indexes flow layers, and \(\phi\) collects their parameters. The final density \(q_\phi=q_{\phi,L}\) is

\[ \log q_{\phi,L}(z_L)=\log q_{\phi,0}(z_0)-\sum_{\ell=1}^L\log|\det DT_{\phi,\ell}(z_{\ell-1})|. \]

Use \(q_{\phi,L}\) in the ordinary ELBO. The new object is a flexible distribution obtained by deforming a simple one, with density evaluation made possible by tractable Jacobian determinants. AEVB already uses a transform to differentiate a sample. Normalizing flows makes the transform itself part of a richer variational family.

A transform changes both shape and density height. In one dimension, \(z_1=2z_0\) doubles the width of every small interval, so the density must halve. The correction is \(-\log2\). In several dimensions, the Jacobian determinant measures the corresponding local volume change. This bookkeeping is what lets a flexible transformed sampler remain usable inside an ELBO that requires \(\log q_\phi\).

A more interesting two-dimensional example is the shear

\[ z_1=u_1,\qquad z_2=u_2+a(u_1^2-1), \qquad u\sim\mathcal N(0,I). \]

It bends a circular Gaussian cloud into a curved distribution. Its Jacobian is triangular with determinant one, so the density at the transformed point is simply the original density at \(u\). The coordinates become dependent even though the base coordinates were independent.

Stacking transformations lets small, tractable deformations build more complicated geometry. The original paper develops planar and radial flows as economical building blocks.

The constraints are computational. A general expressive map may have an expensive determinant or fail to be invertible. Restricted layers make density evaluation feasible but may need substantial depth to fit difficult geometry. A continuous invertible transformation of a full-support Gaussian also cannot create a truly disconnected support, though it can form separated high-density regions joined by low density. IAF chooses a transformation structure suited to efficient variational sampling.

What carries forward. The reusable building block is the change-of-variables identity. A transform used only to differentiate a sample is not the same as a learned invertible map used to enlarge the variational family.

Improved Variational Inference with Inverse Autoregressive Flow

The move. Choose autoregressive transforms with triangular Jacobians and parallel forward sampling.

Read with Normalizing flows · Amortization gap

The idea and the mathematics

Flows are expressive in principle, but a general invertible transform may have an expensive Jacobian determinant or slow sampling. Inverse autoregressive flow chooses a triangular dependency structure so density corrections are cheap and forward sampling can be parallelized.

In a typical autoregressive transform, \(z_i'=m_i(z_{\lt i})+s_i(z_{\lt i})z_i\). The Jacobian is triangular, so its log determinant is \(\sum_i\log|s_i|\). The input vector \(z\) is already available when generating \(z'\), allowing the transform outputs to be computed in parallel using a masked network. Normalizing flows gives the change-of-variables framework. IAF chooses a structure that gives a richer transformation with practical sampling and density evaluation along the forward sampling path.

The direction of computation matters. In two dimensions, write

\[ z'_1=m_1+s_1z_1,\qquad z'_2=m_2(z_1)+s_2(z_1)z_2, \quad s_i\gt 0. \]

The second output depends on the first input, which is already known. A masked network can therefore compute the transform's means and scales from the whole input vector in one pass. The Jacobian has a zero above the diagonal, and its determinant is just \(s_1s_2(z_1)\). Dependencies are allowed without paying for a general determinant.

Along the sampling path, we know every intermediate input and can accumulate the density corrections immediately. This is exactly the operation VI repeatedly needs: draw a sample and evaluate its density. Inverting the map at an arbitrary supplied output is a different operation and can require sequential recovery of coordinates.

Why stack layers? One autoregressive order favors particular dependencies. Multiple transformations, often with changes of coordinate order, can represent a richer posterior. The paper uses gated affine transformations to make deep flows easier to train.

The tradeoff. Every layer adds network computation, and tiny scales can create poor numerical conditioning. Parallel evaluation does not make an arbitrarily deep flow free. A more expressive family can reduce approximation error, but its encoder still has to learn good parameters—precisely the distinction examined in inference suboptimality.

What carries forward. Autoregressive structure here belongs to the approximation architecture, not necessarily to the generative model. The main contribution is a tractable implementation choice within the flow family, with a trade-off between forward and inverse computations.

2015Family · Auxiliary bound

Markov Chain Monte Carlo and Variational Inference: Bridging the Gap

The move. Enrich a variational draw with Markov transitions and introduce a reverse model to form a tractable bound.

Read with Normalizing flows · Hierarchical VI · FIVO

The idea and the mathematics

A flow uses deterministic invertible transformations, making the final density calculable through Jacobians. What if we instead enrich a simple draw with stochastic Markov transitions? Those transitions can improve the final distribution, but integrating over the hidden trajectory makes the final-state density hard to evaluate. This paper introduces a reverse model to repair that accounting problem.

Define a tractable joint law for a sequence \(q(z_{0:T})=q_0(z_0)\prod_t K_t(z_t\mid z_{t-1})\), using Markov transitions to improve the last state. The marginal \(q(z_T)\) can be hard to evaluate. Introduce a tractable reverse model \(r(z_{0:T-1}\mid z_T)\) and optimize

\[ \mathbb E_q[\log \gamma(z_T)+\log r(z_{0:T-1}\mid z_T)-\log q(z_{0:T})]. \]

This is the final-state ELBO minus the expected KL divergence between the true reverse conditional and \(r\). It uses random transitions to enrich the approximation. Normalizing flows uses invertible deterministic transformations whose marginal densities follow directly from the Jacobian.

The final sample hides the route taken to reach it. We can multiply transition probabilities to evaluate an entire trajectory. Evaluating only the final state's marginal would require integrating over all possible earlier states. The reverse model supplies a tractable account of those hidden histories.

The gap is explicit:

\[ \mathcal L[q(z_T)]-\mathcal L_{\mathrm{aux}} =\mathbb E_{q(z_T)}\!\left[ \mathrm{KL}\big(q(z_{0:T-1}\mid z_T)\|r(z_{0:T-1}\mid z_T)\big) \right]. \]

If the reverse model describes the true conditional history perfectly, the auxiliary bound equals the ELBO of the final-state distribution. Otherwise, we optimize a lower bound on that ELBO. This makes stochastic transformations usable without an explicit final-state density.

The paper develops Hamiltonian variational inference: momentum variables and dynamics use the target's geometry to improve an initial draw, while variational training tunes the short trajectory. This shifts the emphasis from running a chain indefinitely to learning a useful finite computation.

Why more transitions need not improve the trained bound. They can enrich the marginal distribution while also making the reverse conditional harder to approximate. A poorly fitted reverse model can hide that gain behind a larger auxiliary gap. Additional transitions cost computation, and the variational construction does not make every finite trajectory an exact posterior draw. Its value is a trainable bridge between simple variational distributions and MCMC-inspired exploration.

What carries forward. The general idea is variational inference on an augmented path space. It makes finite stochastic transformations trainable, without claiming that a short chain has reached stationarity or that the resulting marginal density is explicit.

Hierarchical Variational Models

The move. Mix conditional approximations through shared auxiliary variables and bound the resulting intractable entropy.

Read with MFVI · Normalizing flows · MCMC–VI bridge · Structured SVI · Variational boosting · Copula VI · Semi-implicit VI

The idea and the mathematics

The reverse-model trick need not describe a Markov trajectory. Suppose we first draw a shared auxiliary setting and then independently draw the latent blocks conditional on it. Integrating out the auxiliary setting creates dependence, but also makes the marginal approximation's density harder to evaluate. Hierarchical VI uses an auxiliary bound for that difficulty.

Let \(a\) be the shared auxiliary variable. First draw \(a\), then draw the unknowns conditionally on it; \(\phi\) collects the parameters of this sampling construction.

Let \(q(z)=\int q(z\mid a)q(a)\,da\), with \(q(z\mid a)\) potentially factorized. Integrating out a shared \(a\) can make the coordinates dependent. Since \(\log q(z)\) is hard to compute, introduce \(r(a\mid z)\) and optimize

\[ \mathbb E_{q(z,a)}[\log \gamma(z)+\log r(a\mid z)-\log q(z,a)]. \]

As in MCMC–VI bridge, the gap from the marginal ELBO is an expected KL involving the auxiliary conditional. In MCMC–VI bridge the auxiliary variables encode a trajectory. In Hierarchical VI they index distributions in a mixture.

Conditional independence can produce marginal dependence. Suppose \(a\sim\mathcal N(0,\tau^2)\) and, conditional on \(a\), the coordinates are independently distributed as \(z_i\mid a\sim\mathcal N(a,s^2)\). Although the conditional factors are independent,

\[ \operatorname{Cov}(z_i,z_j) =\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbb E[z_i\mid a],\mathbb E[z_j\mid a]) =\tau^2\quad(i\ne j). \]

A shared random mean moves them together. The same idea can create more complex dependencies by randomizing other parameters of otherwise simple factors. In the paper's construction, the auxiliary hierarchy is part of the approximation; it need not be a latent variable in the original generative model.

This is useful even for discrete unknowns. For example, random shared Bernoulli probabilities can couple bits without applying an invertible continuous flow directly to those bits. The flexibility lives in the distribution over the factor parameters.

The new bottleneck is entropy. Sampling the hierarchy is easy, but integrating it out to compute \(\log q(z)\) can be hard. The reverse distribution \(r(a\mid z)\) makes a bound tractable, at the cost of another approximation to learn. If it poorly identifies which auxiliary settings could have generated a sample, the auxiliary gap can remain large. A richer hierarchy therefore helps only when its flexibility can also be trained effectively.

What carries forward. The shared-variable mixture mechanism and auxiliary conditional bound both transfer beyond the original hierarchical construction. The hierarchy belongs to q; it should not be confused with adding an extra latent variable to the scientific model.

Variational Boosting: Iteratively Refining Posterior Approximations

The move. Expand a fitted approximation by adding mixture components and covariance structure.

Read with Normalizing flows · Hierarchical VI · Pathfinder · Variational tempering · Mixture natural gradients · Boosting as optimization · Black-box boosting

The idea and the mathematics

A single Gaussian may have reached its best ELBO and still be unable to represent two peaks. Variational boosting makes the family grow as computation becomes available.

Starting from a fitted density \(q^{(t)}\), introduce a new component \(r_\psi\) and mixing weight \(a\):

\[ q^{(t+1)}(z)=(1-a)q^{(t)}(z)+a\,r_\psi(z), \qquad 0\leq a\leq1. \]

Optimize the ELBO of this mixture over \(a,\psi\), keeping the existing components fixed during this addition. The paper also develops efficient Gaussian components with diagonal plus low-rank covariance structure.

Add capacity where the current fit is inadequate. Imagine that the existing Gaussian covers the left peak of a bimodal target. Moving its mean to the right would abandon what it already explains. Adding a component gives the approximation another place to put probability while retaining the first peak.

For a fixed candidate component, the initial improvement is

\[ \left.\frac{d}{da}\mathcal L[(1-a)q^{(t)}+ar_\psi]\right|_{a=0} =\mathbb E_{r_\psi}\!\left[\log\frac{\gamma}{q^{(t)}}\right] -\mathbb E_{q^{(t)}}\!\left[\log\frac{\gamma}{q^{(t)}}\right]. \]

This calculation explains the incentive: a candidate helps when it places mass where the target-to-current-fit ratio is relatively large. The finite-step optimization must also account for the mixture's entropy.

Mixture expectations split into component expectations,

\[ \mathbb E_{q^{(t+1)}}[h] =(1-a)\mathbb E_{q^{(t)}}[h]+a\mathbb E_{r_\psi}[h]. \]

This permits pathwise gradients within continuous components without differentiating through a sampled component index. The log density in the ELBO remains the log of the whole mixture.

What changes. The family expands; the target and reverse-KL criterion remain. In an exact optimization, \(a=0\) preserves the previous fit, so adding a component cannot worsen the best achievable ELBO. Numerical training still needs to find a useful component. Poor initialization can repeatedly rediscover the same mode, and density evaluation grows more expensive as components accumulate.

Compare flows, which deform a density, and Pathfinder, which constructs Gaussian candidates from optimization paths.

2017Implicit family and density ratio

Adversarial Variational Bayes: Unifying Variational Autoencoders and Generative Adversarial Networks

The move. Learn the posterior-to-prior density ratio with a discriminator so an implicit encoder can optimize the VAE objective.

Read with AEVB · Normalizing flows · Operator VI · Amortization gap · Semi-implicit VI

The idea and the mathematics

An encoder can easily generate \(z=T_\phi(x,\epsilon)\) while making its density \(q_\phi(z\mid x)\) impossible to evaluate. That blocks the entropy term in the AEVB objective. Adversarial variational Bayes learns the density ratio that the ELBO needs.

Train a discriminator \(D_\psi(x,z)\) to distinguish two equally likely sources: pairs drawn from the encoder, and pairs formed using the same data distribution but an independent prior draw. At its ideal optimum,

\[ D^\star(x,z)=\frac{q_\phi(z\mid x)} {q_\phi(z\mid x)+p(z)}. \]

Its log odds therefore satisfy

\[ T^\star(x,z)=\log\frac{D^\star(x,z)}{1-D^\star(x,z)} =\log q_\phi(z\mid x)-\log p(z). \]

Classification reveals a ratio without evaluating either source through the classifier. If a particular pair is four times as likely under the encoder source as under the prior source, the optimal classifier assigns probability \(4/5\) to the encoder label, and its log odds are \(\log4\).

Substitute that ratio into the familiar objective:

This is useful when a simple Gaussian encoder is too restrictive. Its cost is an additional approximation: a weak or lagging discriminator gives a wrong penalty and misleading gradients. 2016Family + optimization

Stein Variational Gradient Descent: A General Purpose Bayesian Inference Algorithm

The move. Move a collection of particles in a kernel-defined direction that locally decreases KL.

Read with Pathwise transport · Normalizing flows · Operator VI · Kernel SIVI · Accelerating particles · Particle variance & curvature · Kernelized path gradients

The idea and the mathematics

So far we've represented an approximation by a tractable density or a sampler with an augmented bound. SVGD chooses another representation: a collection of interacting particles. Instead of evaluating the empirical density, it derives how a smooth movement of probability mass changes KL and approximates that movement with the particles.

Consider infinitesimally moving samples by \(z\mapsto z+\eta v(z)\). Differentiating \(\mathrm{KL}(q_\eta\|\pi)\) at zero yields the negative expectation under \(q\) of \(\nabla\log\gamma(z)^\top v(z)+\nabla\cdot v(z)\). The paper chooses the steepest direction among vector fields in a kernel function space. With \(K\) particles \(z^{(s)}\), the resulting velocity is

\[ v(z)=\frac1K\sum_{s=1}^K \big[k(z^{(s)},z)\nabla_{z^{(s)}}\log\gamma(z^{(s)})+\nabla_{z^{(s)}}k(z^{(s)},z)\big]. \]

The first term uses the target score and the second accounts for how the transform expands or contracts the distribution. Pathwise transport uses transport to differentiate expectations within a parameterized family. SVGD uses transport to define the inference update itself.

Why particles do not all climb the same hill. The score term moves particles toward regions of higher target density. Used alone, that looks like optimization of points and encourages collapse near a mode. For a Gaussian kernel \(k(a,b)=\exp(-\|a-b\|^2/h)\),

\[ \nabla_a k(a,b)=-\frac2h(a-b)k(a,b). \]

When this contribution from particle \(a\) is evaluated at particle \(b\), it points away from \(a\). The repulsive term encourages spread, reflecting the entropy part of the distributional objective. Attraction and repulsion together aim to approximate a distribution rather than just locate its mode.

With one particle and this kernel, the self-repulsion is zero, and the method reduces to a score-driven point update. Multiple interacting particles are what give it a representation of uncertainty. The unknown normalizing constant disappears because \(\nabla_z\log\pi=\nabla_z\log\gamma\).

Where the particle picture can mislead. The KL descent argument is derived for a smooth transported distribution; a finite empirical collection is its computational approximation, not a collection of independent exact posterior draws. Bandwidth matters: overly local kernels provide little interaction, while overly broad ones can blur useful structure. High-dimensional distances and poorly initialized particles can make exploration difficult. The method provides an alternative representation and update, without automatically resolving missing modes.

What carries forward. Its transport identity uses only the unnormalized target score. The finite particle cloud is not an iid sample from the exact target, and the method's quality depends on kernel choice, initialization, and particle interactions.

The idea and the mathematics

A richer variational family does not guarantee that an inference network will use it well. If the encoder predicts poor local parameters, we need to know whether the limitation is the family or the shared prediction rule. Inference suboptimality separates those failures by comparing the encoder output with the best local fit in the same family.

For each observation, let \(q_x^\star\) be the best member of the chosen local family and \(q_\phi(\cdot\mid x)\) the encoder output. Insert and subtract \(\mathcal L[q_x^\star]\):

\[ \log Z-\mathcal L[q_\phi] =\underbrace{\log Z-\mathcal L[q_x^\star]}_{\text{approximation gap}} +\underbrace{\mathcal L[q_x^\star]-\mathcal L[q_\phi]}_{\text{amortization gap}}. \]

The first improvement diagnoses amortization error; the remaining mismatch suggests a limitation of the family.

The paper operationalizes this distinction by fixing a trained generative model, locally optimizing variational parameters for individual observations, and comparing with the encoder's output. The ideal decomposition uses the best member of the family. In practice, a local optimizer may not find it, so an observed refinement gain measures a recoverable portion of the gap. Also, comparisons should hold the generative model fixed: retraining that model can reshape the posterior and change both gaps at once.

What carries forward. This decomposition is a reusable diagnostic: changing the family targets approximation error; improving inference optimization or the encoder targets amortization error. The two should be measured with the model fixed.

The idea and the mathematics

Once we've identified an amortization gap, the most direct response is to improve each encoder prediction rather than discard the encoder. Semi-amortized VI makes the encoder an initialization for a small local optimizer and trains the initialization through the correction steps.

An encoder gives a fast initial guess. Instead of accepting it as the final local fit, refine it:

\[ \varphi^{(0)}=f_\phi(x),\qquad \varphi^{(t+1)}=\varphi^{(t)}+ \eta_t\nabla_\varphi\mathcal L_x(\varphi^{(t)}). \]

\[ \frac{d\varphi^{(1)}}{d\phi} =\left[I+\eta_0\nabla_\varphi^2\mathcal L_x(\varphi^{(0)})\right] \frac{df_\phi(x)}{d\phi}. \]

Refinement also remains within the selected variational family, so it targets amortization error without removing that family's approximation gap.

The idea and the mathematics

An optimizer may find the best mean-field distribution for a fixed dataset. But does that approximation learn the true underlying parameter as the dataset grows? The consistency question concerns statistical behavior across datasets, not whether a numerical algorithm converges on one dataset.

The global unknown is a block \(z_g\) of \(z\); the question is what happens to its variational posterior as the dataset grows.

Profile out the local variational factors to obtain a variational criterion for the global parameter. Under regularity and local asymptotic normality conditions on this criterion, the appropriately rescaled VB posterior approaches the KL projection of a limiting Gaussian onto the variational family. There are two distinct claims: the unscaled approximation can concentrate at the true parameter, while its local, rescaled covariance can still differ from the limiting Gaussian’s covariance. This is a statistical result as the amount of data grows. Optimization guarantees instead ask how closely an algorithm can approach the variational optimum for a given dataset.

Getting the center right is different from getting uncertainty right. Suppose a two-dimensional posterior becomes approximately Gaussian with covariance

\[ \Sigma_N=\frac1N \begin{pmatrix}1&\rho\\\rho&1\end{pmatrix}. \]

Its reverse-KL projection onto diagonal Gaussians has the same mean, but each variance is \((1-\rho^2)/N\). Both the true posterior and the projection concentrate as \(N\) grows. The paper establishes a variational Bernstein–von Mises theory under assumptions on the profiled variational criterion. After centering and rescaling, the variational posterior behaves like a KL projection of a limiting normal distribution. This gives a route to consistency and asymptotic descriptions, rather than assuming that the computational approximation becomes exact with enough data.

Poor identification, an inconsistent profiled criterion, or other failures of these conditions can invalidate the conclusion. The theory concerns the variational solution itself; an algorithm trapped at a poor local solution is a separate issue. For uncertainty correction at a fitted solution, linear response takes a different route.

What carries forward. The reusable distinction is consistency versus uncertainty calibration. A variational posterior can concentrate at the right point while retaining the wrong rescaled covariance; optimization convergence is a third, separate question.

The idea and the mathematics

Mean field often gives poor covariance estimates even when its means are accurate. Rather than make the approximation itself richer, linear-response VI perturbs the target and asks how the reoptimized means move. Those changes can expose dependencies absent from the fitted factorized density.

Let \(\delta\) be a small perturbation vector. Perturb the log posterior by \(\delta^\top g(z)\). For the exact perturbed posterior, differentiation gives

\[ \left.\frac{d}{d\delta^\top}\mathbb E_{\pi_\delta}[g(z)]\right|_{\delta=0} =\operatorname{Cov}_\pi(g(z)). \]

The paper substitutes the response of reoptimized variational means for this derivative. If \(\phi^\star(\delta)\) solves \(\nabla_\phi\mathcal L_\delta=0\), implicit differentiation computes the response through the Hessian of \(\mathcal L\). The resulting covariance estimate can contain off-diagonal terms even when \(q\) factorizes. It measures how all fitted means move in response to a perturbation, which is a different calculation from taking covariance under the fitted \(q\) directly.

A small push reveals dependence. If increasing preference for larger \(z_1\) also moves the fitted mean of \(z_2\), the model contains a relationship between them, even if the fitted mean-field density has zero covariance. Reoptimizing after the push lets all factors respond together; simply drawing from the original factorized fit cannot reveal that response.

For a Gaussian target \(\mathcal N(m,\Sigma)\), the perturbation \(\delta^\top z\) changes its mean to \(m+\Sigma\delta\). A mean-field Gaussian fit recovers that mean for every nearby perturbation. Differentiating the fitted mean therefore recovers the full \(\Sigma\), including correlations absent from the factorized density. This example shows exactly what the correction measures.

Let \(a(\phi)=\mathbb E_{q_\phi}[g(z)]\) and \(H=\nabla_\phi^2\mathcal L(\phi^\star)\). At a smooth, isolated optimum, implicit differentiation gives the response estimate

\[ \widehat\Sigma_{\mathrm{LR}} =-D_\phi a\;H^{-1}(D_\phi a)^\top. \]

In practice this can be computed through linear solves rather than forming an inverse. The same perturbation idea also measures local sensitivity to prior or model choices.

When to be cautious about the result. Accurate variational means at one point are not enough; their responses to nearby perturbations must be accurate too. A nearly singular Hessian, a change between competing optima, or a poor mean fit can make the correction unreliable. It improves a covariance estimate without turning the fitted \(q\) into a new distribution with those correlations.

What carries forward. This is sensitivity analysis turned into uncertainty estimation. It depends on smooth, stable optima and informative responses, and does not transform the original factorized approximation into a correlated one.

The obstacle. If uncertainty over individual weights is learned, could it also identify which connections are dispensable?

Mathematical move. Use weight-specific multiplicative noise with learned dropout rates in a variational Bayesian formulation. Optimization can drive some weights into very high-noise regimes, making them effectively removable. What changes. Variational weight-family and prior/regularizer design, rather than a general new ELBO identity. A high inferred dropout rate becomes a pruning criterion; posterior uncertainty and model compression meet.

For a weight, the family can be written

\[ q_\phi(w_j)=\mathcal N(m_j,\alpha_j m_j^2). \]

The paper uses an additive-noise parameterization to stabilize gradients when \(\alpha_j\) becomes large. Large noise-to-signal ratios identify weights to prune. This choice is central to the sparsity effect, not a generic property of Bayesian weight uncertainty.

Variational Bayesian Last Layers

The move. Use deterministic variational objectives for sampling-free Bayesian last-layer training.

Read with Bayes by Backprop · Sparse variational dropout · Collapsed optimization

The idea and the mathematics

A fully Bayesian neural network places uncertainty over every weight. A Bayesian last layer instead learns features \(h_\theta(x)\) and places a variational distribution over the final linear map. This paper makes that compromise especially cheap by deriving deterministic, sampling-free variational training objectives.

For scalar Gaussian regression, take \(q_\phi(w)=\mathcal N(m,\Sigma)\), prediction \(w^\top h\), and observation noise variance \(\sigma^2\). The expected squared error is exactly

\[ \mathbb E_{q_\phi}(y-w^\top h)^2=(y-m^\top h)^2+h^\top\Sigma h. \]

Therefore the expected log likelihood is

\[ -\tfrac12\log(2\pi\sigma^2)-\frac{(y-m^\top h)^2+h^\top\Sigma h}{2\sigma^2}. \]

Sum these terms and subtract the Gaussian weight KL: there is no need to sample final-layer weights. The additional variance term teaches the features and weight distribution to account for uncertainty rather than only mean prediction. Classification uses tractable variational bounds rather than this Gaussian regression identity.

What changes. The contribution is more than restricting uncertainty to the final layer: analytic expectations and bounds enable single-pass training and evaluation, with quadratic cost in last-layer width. The paper also combines this with Bayesian feature learning, analytically removing a source of weight-sampling noise.

For deterministic features, uncertainty about the feature extractor remains unrepresented. A confidently wrong representation can therefore still yield misleading predictions. Learning \(\theta\) alongside \(\phi\) does not make those feature parameters Bayesian.
